# Epidemiological surveillance and syndromic outbreak detection

## Syndromic surveillance and aberration detection algorithms
**Term of art:** Aberration detection (also: EARS — Early Aberration Reporting System; CUSUM — cumulative sum control chart)
Automated systems ingest high-frequency, pre-diagnostic data — emergency department chief complaints, over-the-counter pharmacy sales, school absenteeism rates, 911 call categories — and run statistical algorithms (typically CUSUM or scan statistics) that flag when a signal deviates from a modeled baseline. The baseline itself is seasonally adjusted to avoid flagging normal flu-season rises. The human epidemiologist does not monitor raw streams; they receive only exceedances — cases where the algorithm determines the deviation is improbable under normal conditions. The signal that rises is already a compressed judgment: not "here is data" but "here is an anomaly requiring investigation."
**Failure mode:** The algorithm flags without context. An aberration in respiratory chief complaints might be a bad data feed, a local sports event, or anthrax — the system cannot distinguish. Without a human rapid investigation protocol triggered by every alert, the alert queue becomes noise, epidemiologists habituate, and true signals are missed in the same moment they are technically detected (alarm fatigue variant).

## Case definition and the line list
**Term of art:** Case definition; line list
A case definition is a precise, pre-negotiated rule — specifying clinical criteria, lab confirmation requirements, time window, and geographic scope — that determines whether a reported event counts as a case of the condition under surveillance. Epidemiologists maintain a line list: a structured, living spreadsheet in which each row is one confirmed or suspected case and columns are epidemiologically meaningful attributes (onset date, exposure history, symptom profile, geographic location). The line list is not a passive record; it is the working surface on which the outbreak is understood. Updating it is itself an analytical act — every new case added either extends or constrains the hypothesis about the source.
**Failure mode:** Case definitions set too loosely generate noise that overwhelms investigation capacity; set too tightly, they systematically miss atypical presentations. The failure is a threshold-calibration problem: the definition encodes an implicit prior about what the outbreak looks like before you know what the outbreak looks like.

## Epi curve construction and real-time re-interpretation
**Term of art:** Epidemic curve (epi curve); index case; incubation period bracketing
The epi curve is a histogram of case onset dates. Its shape encodes mechanistic information: a sharp peak suggests a point source exposure; a propagating wave suggests person-to-person transmission; a plateau suggests ongoing common-source exposure. Epidemiologists continuously redraw the epi curve as new cases are confirmed and onset dates are revised. The curve is a hypothesis-display device — the human's task is to look at its shape and ask "what exposure pattern would produce this?" The incubation period of the suspected pathogen is used to bracket backward from the peak to identify the probable exposure window, creating an actionable time range for source investigation.
**Failure mode:** Right-truncation bias. Because cases near the present have not yet fully manifested or been reported, the current tail of the epi curve systematically underestimates case counts. Investigators reading the raw curve may believe an outbreak is declining when it is not. Failure to account for reporting lag produces false reassurance and premature stand-down.

## Contact tracing and the exposure network map
**Term of art:** Contact tracing; attack rate; secondary attack rate
For each confirmed case, a structured interview elicits every person who had sufficient contact with the case during their infectious period to warrant monitoring for infection. These contacts are then followed (passively or actively) for the duration of the incubation period. The secondary attack rate — the proportion of contacts who develop disease — is a field-measured estimate of transmissibility. The network of who-infected-whom, when mappable, becomes a directed graph that reveals whether transmission chains are branching (uncontrolled spread) or terminating (containment working). Human investigators are the sensing layer here; the system's role is to track which contacts have been reached, which are pending, and which have cleared their incubation window.
**Failure mode:** Contact tracing collapses under case load. It is designed for containment of small clusters; when case counts exceed investigator capacity, contacts go un-followed, chains go unbroken, and the method that was providing ground truth about transmission dynamics goes silent exactly when ground truth is most needed. This is the scale-cliff failure mode.

## Sentinel surveillance networks
**Term of art:** Sentinel site; sentinel provider; ILINet (Influenza-Like Illness Network)
Rather than attempting to monitor all cases everywhere, sentinel surveillance designates a small, strategically chosen set of reporting sites — clinics, hospitals, laboratories — that report with high fidelity and speed. The premise is that a representative sample, reported reliably, is more actionable than universal but spotty coverage. U.S. ILINet, for example, uses a network of sentinel providers who report weekly counts of influenza-like illness as a proportion of total visits. The signal from sentinels is used to detect national trends, not to count every case. The human using the system must always hold in mind that sentinel data is a proxy — interpretable as a trend signal, not as a census.
**Failure mode:** Sentinel sites are not always geographically or demographically representative. An outbreak that begins in a population or geography outside the sentinel network is invisible until it reaches a sentinel site. The system systematically has blind spots whose location is known in advance but whose activation is unpredictable.

## Situation report cadence and the incident command structure
**Term of art:** Sitrep (situation report); ICS (Incident Command System); Unified Command
During a declared outbreak, information management shifts from passive surveillance to active incident command. Situation reports — structured documents produced on a fixed cadence (often every 12 or 24 hours) — compress the current state of the outbreak into a decision-relevant summary for the command level: case counts, attack rates, intervention coverage, resource status, and current unknowns. The ICS model separates Operations (responding), Intelligence/Surveillance (knowing), and Command (deciding) into defined roles with explicit information handoffs. The sitrep is the artifact that crosses from Intelligence to Command — it is not a data dump but a curated brief with explicit action-forcing questions for the decision-maker.
**Failure mode:** The sitrep cadence creates a decision rhythm that can lag the outbreak rhythm. A fast-moving event may change materially in the 12 hours between sitreps, leaving the commander acting on stale state. The ritual of the fixed-cadence report, designed for manageability, can produce a false sense that the situation is stable between reports.

## Laboratory confirmation threshold and the presumptive/confirmed distinction
**Term of art:** Presumptive positive; confirmed case; presumptive action
Epidemiological response cannot wait for full laboratory confirmation, which may take days. The field operates on a two-tier evidence standard: presumptive positive (preliminary test result consistent with diagnosis, sufficient to trigger response actions) and confirmed (definitive laboratory verification). Response protocols specify which actions are authorized at each tier. This distinction lets the human decision-maker act early without being reckless — the presumptive threshold is calibrated so that the cost of acting on a false presumptive is lower than the cost of waiting for confirmation during a true outbreak.
**Failure mode:** When the presumptive threshold is poorly calibrated or the test has low positive predictive value in a low-prevalence setting, the system generates clusters of presumptive positives that require costly response and then resolve as false alarms. This burns response capacity and, if it recurs, erodes responder trust in the alert system — the same alarm fatigue dynamic as clinical monitoring, but at public health scale.

## What this field knows
Epidemiological surveillance uniquely understands that the human decision-maker's job is not to monitor data but to interrogate a compressed, already-interpreted signal — and that the quality of compression matters as much as the quality of the underlying data. The field has learned that the gap between detection and action is a sociotechnical design problem, not a data problem: you can have the signal and still fail to close the loop if the artifact that carries the signal (the sitrep, the epi curve, the case definition) does not explicitly surface the action-forcing question. It also knows that every surveillance system has a designed scale cliff — a point at which case load, geographic spread, or transmission speed exceeds the human-in-the-loop capacity — and that knowing where that cliff is, before you reach it, is itself a form of situational awareness.
