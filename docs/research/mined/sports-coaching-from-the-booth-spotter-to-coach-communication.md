# Sports coaching from the booth / spotter-to-coach communication
> Mined via open-learning re-run (Sonnet).

## Spotter Call
**Term of art:** Spotter call / spotter report
The spotter sits in the press box or an elevated position with a full-field view that the sideline coach cannot physically have. On every play, the spotter watches the entire defensive formation or offensive alignment and relays what the sideline coach cannot see — coverage shells, blitz packages, personnel groupings mismatches — via a direct headset channel. The communication is structured: the spotter names the observable fact in agreed-upon shorthand ("Cover-2, safety rolled up left"), not an interpretation or recommendation. The coach on the sideline then makes the decision about what to call next.
**Failure mode:** Spotter begins editorializing rather than observing — saying "I think you should run the ball" instead of "linebacker is 8 yards off the line." When the spotter's role drifts from perception to decision, the coach loses the raw signal and can no longer apply their own judgment to it.

## Wristband Call Sheet
**Term of art:** Wristband / call sheet
The offensive coordinator distills the full playbook into a laminated card worn on the quarterback's wrist or held by the sideline coach. It compresses hundreds of possible plays into ~40-60 numbered or coded calls prioritized for the specific opponent game-plan. The wristband is a pre-negotiated compression schema: both parties (coach and QB) share the encoding, so a two-digit number transmitted under crowd noise maps unambiguously to a full play package. The card is rebuilt weekly for each opponent.
**Failure mode:** The sheet becomes too long or uses inconsistent codes, collapsing under game-speed pressure. The compression fails not because the information is wrong but because the encoding is not drilled enough for both parties to share it fluently under noise and time pressure.

## Two-Minute Warning Timeout Protocol
**Term of art:** Two-minute drill / sideline meeting
At natural breaks — timeouts, two-minute warnings, halftime — the offensive coordinator and head coach conduct a structured rapid review using the printed play sheet. They do not review everything; they use the break to re-anchor on a pre-selected priority list ("our top three red-zone plays we haven't hit yet") while the coordinator relays what adjustments the defense has made. The protocol is structured around a known agenda so the conversation does not become a free-form status review.
**Failure mode:** The break becomes consumed by reviewing what already happened (post-mortem drift) rather than pre-loading decisions for what comes next. The human's scarce attention window closes before forward-looking decisions are locked.

## Headset Discipline / Channel Separation
**Term of art:** Headset channels / offensive-defensive channel split
NFL and college staffs run separate headset channels: offensive coaches hear only the offensive coordinator's voice (plus QB feedback); defensive coaches hear only the defensive coordinator. The head coach can monitor both or toggle. This is active noise architecture: by structuring who can broadcast to whom, the system prevents the perceptual overload of every coach hearing every signal. The producer of each channel is a single caller; everyone else is listen-only.
**Failure mode:** Channel contamination — multiple voices breaking in during critical play-call windows. When more than one person can transmit, the human decision-maker receives simultaneous competing signals and cannot parse them. The single-caller rule is the guard.

## Formation Recognition Flashcard Drilling
**Term of art:** Film study / formation recognition reps
Before the game, coaches run players and coordinators through rapid-recognition drills using still frames or short film clips of the upcoming opponent's formations. The goal is to pre-load pattern recognition so that in-game the spotter or the player does not have to consciously reason from raw data — they pattern-match to a pre-built category. When the spotter says "they're in their 4-2-5 nickel," the coach already has a pre-loaded decision tree for it.
**Failure mode:** The opponent shows a formation not drilled in advance, and the pre-loaded pattern library fails to match. The system's pre-built categories create a blind spot for novelty — the human reverts to slower deliberate reasoning precisely when speed is most required.

## Halftime Adjustment Board
**Term of art:** Halftime adjustments / grease board
At halftime, the coordinator produces a structured document (physical whiteboard or printed sheet) that presents the first-half data in a decision-forcing format: what the opponent is doing more than expected, what the team's own calls have not worked, and a small set of specific adjustments. The format is intentionally asymmetric — observations on one side, proposed adjustments on the other — so the coach can see signal-to-action pairs rather than raw statistics.
**Failure mode:** The board is overloaded with data rather than filtered to decision-relevant observations. When the coordinator presents everything they noticed, the coach cannot isolate the 2-3 things that actually require a decision, and halftime ends without committed adjustments.

## Red Zone Package Pre-commitment
**Term of art:** Red zone package / situational scripting
Coaches pre-commit specific play packages to specific game situations before the game — red zone, third-and-short, two-point conversions — and these packages are rehearsed until execution is automatic. This is decision pre-loading: rather than asking the coordinator to generate a play under time pressure when the team crosses the 20-yard line, the decision was already made in the film room. The in-game trigger is recognizing the situation, not generating a response.
**Failure mode:** The pre-committed package is drawn from last week's opponent scouting and does not account for what this week's defense is actually showing. The pre-loading eliminates cognitive load but also eliminates in-game adaptation unless the coordinator has a clear override protocol.

## What this field knows
Sports coaching from the booth understands that the human decision-maker's attention is a non-renewable resource within each possession, and that the system's job is to pre-negotiate shared encodings so that communication at game speed is pattern-matching rather than reasoning. The field also discovered that perceptual superiority in the booth only creates value when the spotter's role is strictly constrained to observation — not interpretation — so the human receives raw signal rather than pre-digested conclusions. Most distinctively, coaching staffs solve the "too much to surface" problem not by filtering in real time but by pre-loading decision packages before the game so that in-game signals need only trigger a pre-made choice rather than generate a new one.
