# Notification science and interruptibility / attention management
> Mined via open-learning re-run (Sonnet).

## Breakpoint Detection
**Term of art:** Opportunistic interruption / interruptibility modeling
The field found that the cost of an interruption is not fixed — it depends on where in a task the human is when the interrupt arrives. Interruptions at natural "breakpoints" (end of a subtask, between cognitive chunks) impose dramatically lower resumption costs than mid-task interruptions. Systems implementing this pattern monitor behavioral or contextual proxies for breakpoint state (idle time, application switching, message-send events) and defer notifications until a breakpoint is detected. The seminal Iqbal & Bailey (2005–2008) line of work quantified resumption lag and showed breakpoint-targeted delivery cut disruption roughly in half compared to immediate delivery.
**Failure mode:** Breakpoint starvation — if the human never reaches a clean breakpoint (deep flow states, emergencies), the deferred notification never fires or fires too late; urgency must override the mechanism.

## Notification Bundling / Digesting
**Term of art:** Notification digest / batching cadence
Rather than firing an alert per event, the system accumulates events over a time window and delivers one consolidated message at a scheduled delivery point. Email digest products (daily/weekly summaries), Slack's "do not disturb" with queued delivery, and GitHub's notification grouping by repository all use this mechanism. The digest collapses N interruptions into 1 while preserving the full information set. The design decision is the cadence (fixed schedule vs. threshold-triggered: "deliver when 10 items queue") and the summarization granularity (item list vs. aggregated counts vs. synthesized narrative).
**Failure mode:** Urgency blindness — the batch treats a P0 alert the same as a low-priority FYI; without urgency-aware escape hatches, critical signals are delayed to the next digest window.

## Ambient / Peripheral Display
**Term of art:** Peripheral awareness / calm technology
Weiser and Brown's 1996 "calm technology" framework distinguished center-of-attention interaction from periphery-of-attention awareness. Ambient displays encode system state in perceptible but non-demanding channels — color, light intensity, spatial position, sound texture — that humans can monitor without switching attentional focus. The classic artifact is the "Dangling String" installation (a spinning wire whose speed encodes network traffic) and, commercially, status-light systems (Luxaflux, Hue integrations for build status). The mechanism is perceptual encoding that stays below the threshold of cognitive interruption until state changes enough that the human's peripheral system pulls it into focus.
**Failure mode:** Encoding collapse — peripheral channels have very low bandwidth; when more than ~3 states need representation the encoding becomes ambiguous and the human either ignores it or must context-switch to decode it, defeating the purpose.

## Alert Fatigue Management / Alarm Rationalization
**Term of art:** Alert fatigue / alarm flood
Intensive-care and industrial control systems documented that when alarm density exceeds roughly 1–2 actionable alarms per 10 minutes, operators enter a suppression mode: they acknowledge without acting, silence without reading, or develop learned inattention to entire alarm classes. The EEMUA 191 standard and ISA-18.2 (Management of Alarm Systems for the Process Industries) codify rationalization procedures: alarm rationalization workshops, consequence-severity matrices, and suppression rules that silence downstream alarms when an upstream root-cause alarm is already active (alarm shelving). The mechanism is a periodic audit cycle that measures alarm rate, classifies each alarm by consequence and operator action, and deletes or suppresses alarms that cannot be acted on.
**Failure mode:** Rationalization theater — the audit removes low-consequence alarms but leaves high-frequency nuisance alarms from instrumentation noise; the rate metric improves while the signal-to-noise experienced by the operator does not.

## Escalation Ladders
**Term of art:** Escalation policy / on-call rotation
Incident management systems (PagerDuty, Opsgenie, VictorOps) codify a tiered routing structure: an alert fires to primary on-call; if unacknowledged within N minutes, it escalates to secondary; then to a manager; then to a broadcast channel. Each tier is a human-system role with defined acknowledgment SLAs. The mechanism converts a binary "alert fired / not fired" into a timed sequence of routing decisions with explicit handoff accountability. Crucially, the escalation ladder also encodes who is the correct decision-maker for each severity level — not just "someone respond" but "the right someone."
**Failure mode:** Escalation fatigue at the top — when primary responders routinely let alerts escalate because escalation is perceived as lower-cost than waking up, the manager layer becomes the de facto primary, collapsing the tiering.

## Attentional Context Preservation / Resumption Cues
**Term of art:** Resumption lag / prospective memory support
When a human is interrupted and must return to a prior task, they suffer resumption lag — the time and error cost of reconstructing what they were doing and where they were. Research by Monk, Trafton, and Boehm-Davis showed that providing a "resumption cue" — a visible artifact encoding the interrupted task's state at the moment of interruption — cuts resumption lag by 40–60%. Practical implementations include the "suspend/resume" card pinned by task management systems, the "draft" auto-saved at interruption time, or the "you were here" breadcrumb shown when re-entering a workflow. The mechanism externalizes prospective memory so the human does not have to hold the interrupted task state in working memory through the interruption.
**Failure mode:** Stale cue — if the system state has changed substantially during the interruption, the resumption cue describes a world that no longer exists, and acting on it causes errors; the cue needs a staleness signal.

## Triage Protocol / Severity Classification
**Term of art:** Triage / severity taxonomy (P0–P4, SEV1–SEV5)
Emergency medicine's triage protocol — invented to handle more casualties than available medical attention — classifies incoming cases into urgency tiers so that scarce attention is allocated by expected benefit, not arrival order. Software incident management imported this directly: severity levels (SEV1 = production down, customer-impacting; SEV5 = cosmetic) pre-answer the question "does this need me now?" before the human evaluates individual events. The classification is done by the system (or by a designated triage role) so the decision-maker receives pre-classified inputs rather than raw events. The mechanism offloads the meta-question ("is this worth my attention?") from the principal actor to an earlier, cheaper stage.
**Failure mode:** Severity inflation — reporters learn that higher severity gets faster response, so they over-classify; over time SEV1 becomes meaningless and the human must re-triage everything, restoring the cognitive load the taxonomy was meant to eliminate.

## Do Not Disturb / Focus Mode Contracts
**Term of art:** Focus mode / DND / flow-state protection
Operating systems and communication platforms now expose explicit machine-readable "focus state" signals (iOS Focus, Windows Focus Assist, Slack status, Google Calendar focus-time blocks). The mechanism is a contract: the human declares a protected window and the system suppresses non-urgent notifications for that window, routing them to a queue. Urgency escape hatches (allowed senders, breakthrough threshold) ensure the protection is not absolute. The novel element vs. simple DND is that focus state is published to other systems — a calendar focus block can suppress Slack, email, and monitoring alerts simultaneously, creating a coherent protected window across channels.
**Failure mode:** Trust erosion — each system that violates the focus contract (fires anyway) reduces the human's willingness to rely on the protection, leading them to disable focus mode entirely; the system's reliability is binary, not gradual.

## What this field knows
Notification science discovered that the cost of information delivery is not the information itself but the moment and form of delivery — the same signal can be negligible or catastrophic depending on where in a cognitive task it lands. The field learned that humans are not passive receivers who can be tuned by adjusting volume; they adapt to overload by suppression, which is often worse than no notification at all. Its deepest insight is that acting through a perceptually-superior system requires the system to model the human's current attentional state, not just the event's urgency — and that without this modeling, more signals produce less response. The practical residue is a set of mechanisms that interpose a deliberate shaping layer between raw events and human attention: batching, escalation, triage, breakpoint detection, and ambient encoding each implement a different answer to "what should surface, when, and in what form."
