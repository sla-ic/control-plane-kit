## Sonification / the variable-pitch pulse oximeter beep — encoding two life-critical variables into a single continuous sound so vigilance runs in the ears while the eyes are busy elsewhere.

**Term of art:** Variable-pitch pulse oximetry / auditory display / sonification; 'the pitch is dropping'

The pulse oximeter does not just display SpO2; it emits a beep per heartbeat whose PITCH rises and falls with oxygen saturation (higher pitch = higher SpO2) and whose RATE tracks heart rate. The anesthesiologist never has to look at the monitor to know oxygenation is falling — a descending pitch is heard pre-attentively, even mid-conversation or while looking into the surgical field. This is a deliberate channel choice: audition is an always-on, peripheral, omnidirectional sense that does not compete with the visual workload. It offloads a continuous-monitoring task from scarce foveal attention to 'free' background perception. The encoding is also ABSOLUTE-from-relative: clinicians can detect the DIRECTION and RATE of desaturation from pitch change long before they could read and interpret a number.

**Failure mode:** Studies show the pitch-discrimination signal degrades sharply with ambient OR noise, music, and competing alarms; in noisy rooms clinicians under-detect moderate desaturation. Over-reliance means a muted or disconnected speaker silently removes the whole channel. The encoding is also non-linear and only well-discriminated in certain ranges, so subtle mid-range drift can be missed by ear.

## Graded alarm urgency mapping — encoding HOW MUCH a derangement deserves attention into the physical character of the signal (the IEC 60601-1-8 alarm standard).

**Term of art:** IEC 60601-1-8; high/medium/low priority alarms; alarm 'urgency mapping'; auditory icons / 'earcons'

Anesthesia/OR alarms are not binary. International standard IEC 60601-1-8 defines a deliberate auditory and visual grammar: HIGH priority (red, fast-flashing ~1.4-2.8 Hz, a 10-pulse melodic burst that repeats urgently) = act NOW / immediately life-threatening; MEDIUM priority (yellow, slower flash, 3-pulse burst) = prompt response needed; LOW priority (cyan/steady, 1-2 pulses or just a visual) = awareness, no immediate action. Urgency is mapped to tempo, pitch, pulse-count, repetition interval, and color so the human can triage by EAR ALONE without reading text — the form of the signal carries the priority. The standard even reserves specific melodic 'signature' tunes for categories (e.g., cardiovascular, ventilation, oxygenation) so the family of the problem is partly encoded in the sound.

**Failure mode:** The standardized melodic alarms are notoriously hard to learn and discriminate — studies show even experienced anesthesiologists identify the standard tunes at rates barely above chance, so the 'family' information is largely lost. Manufacturers also set defaults conservatively, inflating priority, which trains staff to distrust the grading. The result is the grammar exists on paper but collapses into 'noise' in practice.

## Alarm fatigue and the deliberate engineering against it — the field's central, named failure mode of an over-perceiving system.

**Term of art:** Alarm fatigue; nuisance/false-positive alarms; alarm delay; smart alarms / alarm integration; 'crying wolf'

Anesthesiology was among the first to name and study ALARM FATIGUE: when 85-99% of monitor alarms are false or clinically insignificant, the human's response to ALL alarms degrades — they silence, lower volume, widen limits, or stop attending. This is the canonical pathology of a system that perceives more than the human can act on. The field's countermeasures are concrete and instructive: (1) ALARM DELAYS — a transient SpO2 dip must persist N seconds before annunciating, filtering artifact; (2) SMART/MEDIAN alarms that require a parameter to stay out of range, not just touch it; (3) intelligent limit-setting tied to patient baseline rather than factory defaults; (4) 'SmartAlarms' that FUSE multiple parameters before firing (e.g., low BP + tachycardia + falling EtCO2 together = one meaningful 'possible hypovolemia/embolism' alert instead of three separate nuisance alarms). The doctrine: the goal is not more alarms but a higher SIGNAL-TO-NOISE ratio so each annunciation re-earns the human's attention.

**Failure mode:** The countermeasures cut both ways: delays and integration introduce LATENCY and can suppress a true rapid event; widening limits to reduce noise can silence a real derangement; and 'alarm silence/pause' buttons meant to be temporary get left permanently engaged. The deeper failure is that desensitization is partly irreversible — once a human learns an alarm class is usually wrong, even a redesigned version inherits the distrust.

## The integrated 'visual field' display and pre-attentive encoding — designing the screen so deviation POPS without reading (Drager/GE displays, and the research 'graphical/object displays').

**Term of art:** Pre-attentive processing; ecological interface design (EID); object/configural displays; the capnogram waveform morphology; 'gestalt' monitoring

Modern anesthesia monitors lay out waveforms and numbers in a FIXED spatial grammar (ECG always top, SpO2 plethysmograph below, capnogram in a known place, color-coded by parameter — red for arterial, blue/cyan for SpO2, yellow for EtCO2 conventionally) so the clinician builds a stable mental map and detects change by SHAPE and POSITION, not by reading. Research 'ecological interface design' / object displays go further: they render the patient's physiology as an integrated GRAPHICAL OBJECT (e.g., a polygon whose shape distorts when a variable leaves normal, or a 'histogram of normality') so a single abnormal variable visibly breaks an otherwise symmetric gestalt — exploiting pre-attentive vision (the deviation is seen in <200ms before conscious search). The capnogram waveform itself is a famous information-dense object: its SHAPE (not a number) instantly distinguishes bronchospasm (shark-fin upslope), rebreathing (rising baseline), disconnection (flat line), or cardiac oscillations.

**Failure mode:** Fixed layouts breed CHANGE BLINDNESS and inattentional blindness — clinicians 'looking at' the monitor miss a parameter that drifted because nothing 'popped'; the famous result is that anesthesiologists can fail to notice a flatlined waveform during high workload. Novel object displays improve detection in studies but transfer poorly: they require training, and under stress people revert to the numbers they trust.

## Escalation from passive monitor to active intervention — the doctrine of WHEN the human must stop watching and act, and how the loop hands off.

**Term of art:** 'Treat the patient, not the monitor'; escalation ladder / stepwise management; trajectory vs threshold; 'getting behind' the patient

Anesthesiology has explicit teaching on the transition from VIGILANCE (passive scanning) to INTERVENTION. It is taught as a hierarchy: first TREAT THE PATIENT, NOT THE MONITOR (verify the derangement is real — is the pulse ox probe off? is the BP cuff cycling on a leaning arm? — before acting), then a stepped escalation. Hypotension, for example, has a near-reflexive escalation ladder (confirm reading -> check rhythm/HR -> assess volume/anesthetic depth -> vasopressor bolus -> infusion -> call for help -> consider differential: hemorrhage, embolism, anaphylaxis, ischemia). The key doctrine is that escalation is TRIGGERED BY PATTERN AND TRAJECTORY, not a single threshold: a value heading the wrong way fast outranks a worse-but-stable value. And there is a hard rule that recognizing you are BEHIND the patient (losing the loop) is itself a trigger to escalate to calling for help.

**Failure mode:** FIXATION ERROR (see below) and anchoring: once committed to one intervention/diagnosis the clinician keeps pushing it past the point it's working. The opposite failure is premature intervention on artifact (chasing a false low BP with pressors). And the handoff from monitoring to acting collapses attention — while intervening, the human stops scanning the OTHER streams, so a second derangement is missed.

## Crisis Resource Management (CRM) and cognitive aids / emergency manuals — converting a RECOGNIZED state into a scripted JOINT procedure across the team.

**Term of art:** Crisis Resource Management (CRM); closed-loop communication; 'declare an emergency'; cognitive aids / emergency manuals; the 'reader' role; designated event manager

Borrowed directly from aviation Crew Resource Management, anesthesia CRM is the doctrine for the moment after a crisis is recognized: it redistributes the now-overwhelming perception-and-action load across a TEAM with explicit roles. Core moves: DECLARE AN EMERGENCY out loud (name the state — 'this is malignant hyperthermia'), assign a team LEADER who steps back from hands-on tasks to maintain the global picture (deliberately PROTECTING one person's attention as the integrator), use CLOSED-LOOP COMMUNICATION (order -> read-back -> confirmation of completion) so no instruction is dropped, and call for help EARLY. Paired with this are EMERGENCY MANUALS / COGNITIVE AIDS (e.g., the Stanford Emergency Manual, ASA crisis checklists): physical or on-screen checklists for ~25 OR crises (anaphylaxis, malignant hyperthermia, local-anesthetic systemic toxicity, cardiac arrest, can't-intubate-can't-oxygenate) that a designated READER runs aloud so the scripted steps replace fallible working memory under stress.

**Failure mode:** Manuals are under-used precisely when needed — under stress, teams forget the manual exists or feel that stopping to read it costs time (the 'I don't have time to check the checklist' paradox). Closed-loop communication degrades as stress rises (orders shouted to no one in particular). And declaring an emergency carries social friction — hierarchy and ego suppress the early call for help, the single most studied CRM failure.

## Closed-loop CONTROL the machine may own vs. decisions the human must own — the explicit boundary of delegated autonomy.

**Term of art:** Closed-loop anesthesia delivery (CLAD); supervisory control; setpoint/target; automation 'levels'; human-on-the-loop vs in-the-loop

Anesthesia is one of the few clinical fields with REAL closed-loop control systems in practice and research: closed-loop anesthesia delivery (CLAD) where the machine titrates propofol/remifentanil to a depth-of-anesthesia target (BIS setpoint), closed-loop fluid administration to a stroke-volume goal, and closed-loop oxygen/FiO2 control to an SpO2 target. The doctrine is sharply drawn about WHAT the loop may own: it may own the high-frequency, well-defined REGULATION task (keep BIS at 50, keep SpO2 at 96%) — a control problem with a clear setpoint and fast feedback — because a controller does this more steadily than a distractible human. But the machine does NOT own the SUPERVISORY decisions: choosing the target, deciding the patient's state has changed such that the target itself is wrong, recognizing the controller is fighting an artifact, or declaring the situation a crisis. The human is the SUPERVISORY CONTROLLER over an automated regulator. This maps exactly to 'agent owns the loop, human owns the goal and the exceptions.'

**Failure mode:** The classic automation ironies: (1) AUTOMATION COMPLACENCY — the supervisor stops monitoring the regulator that 'always works,' so when it fails on an edge case the human is out of the loop and slow to retake control; (2) SKILL ATROPHY — manual titration skill decays; (3) the controller faithfully chasing a CORRUPTED INPUT (a BIS contaminated by EMG/artifact) and confidently driving the patient to a dangerous state. The named risk is being 'a passenger' rather than a supervisor.

## Fixation error and the structured re-scan / 'step back' — named cognitive failure modes of attention and the drilled countermeasures.

**Term of art:** Fixation error (this-and-only-this / everything-but-this / everything's-OK); the 'step back'; structured scan; cross-monitoring; 'fresh eyes'

Anesthesiology has a precise taxonomy of how a saturated human's attention fails, taught explicitly so it can be countered: 'THIS AND ONLY THIS' (locking onto one diagnosis), 'EVERYTHING BUT THIS' (excluding the real cause), and 'EVERYTHING'S OK' (denial that a crisis is occurring). The drilled countermeasures are procedural attention-resets: the deliberate STEP BACK / 'big picture' pause (literally taking hands off and re-scanning all streams from scratch), the structured periodic SCAN (a disciplined sweep of monitor-patient-surgical field-machine in a fixed order, like an instrument scan in flying), saying findings ALOUD to force re-examination, and inviting a fresh set of eyes ('does anyone see something I'm missing?') because a person not anchored to the original frame breaks fixation. The field treats the SECOND OPINION as an attention-correction instrument, not just expertise.

**Failure mode:** The countermeasures depend on the very metacognition that stress destroys — a fixated person by definition doesn't feel fixated, so they don't trigger their own step-back. Cross-monitoring fails under steep hierarchy (juniors won't challenge a senior's frame) and when the whole team shares the same wrong frame (collective fixation), which no internal scan can break.

## Pre-induction checklists and challenge-response — front-loading vigilance to prevent the predictable derangement before it can occur.

**Term of art:** Anesthesia machine check; pre-induction checklist; WHO Surgical Safety Checklist; 'time-out'; challenge-response; 'sign-in / time-out / sign-out'

Much of anesthesia safety is moved UPSTREAM of monitoring: the machine check, the pre-induction checklist, and the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist 'time-out' (challenge-response confirmation of patient, procedure, site, allergies, airway risk, blood availability before incision). The logic is that the cheapest derangement to catch is one prevented by verifying state BEFORE the high-workload phase begins, when attention is still abundant. Challenge-response format (one person reads the item, another confirms the actual state) is chosen specifically because it defeats the 'sense of having checked' — it forces an external verification rather than a memory. This is the field's answer to 'what should rise to attention when the human is NOT yet saturated': a fixed, exhaustive, low-stakes-moment verification of the things that will be lethal and invisible later (e.g., is the backup oxygen connected, is suction working, is the airway plan agreed).

**Failure mode:** Checklists decay into ROTE RITUAL — read aloud while no one verifies the actual state ('checklist theater'), so the form survives but the verification dies. Over-long lists get truncated under time pressure. And they only cover ANTICIPATED failure modes; a novel or off-list hazard passes a perfectly completed checklist.

## Capnography as the single most trusted 'ground-truth' channel — the field's doctrine that one stream can confirm or veto all the others.

**Term of art:** Capnography / end-tidal CO2 (EtCO2); 'no trace, wrong place'; sudden loss of capnogram; ETCO2 as a marker of return of spontaneous circulation

Anesthesiology elevated end-tidal CO2 (capnography) to a privileged status as the fastest, hardest confirmation of the two things that kill quickest: is the airway actually connected and ventilating, and is there circulation. A sudden LOSS of the CO2 waveform is treated as an emergency until proven otherwise because it has a short, lethal differential (disconnection, esophageal intubation, total airway obstruction, or cardiac arrest / loss of pulmonary blood flow). It is doctrine that capnography CONFIRMS endotracheal tube placement ('no trace, wrong place') over and above clinical judgment, and that during CPR a rising EtCO2 is the real-time signal that circulation has returned. The field deliberately designates a SINGLE authoritative channel for the highest-stakes question so that when streams conflict, there is a known tiebreaker rather than paralysis.

**Failure mode:** Even the trusted channel has artifact and blind spots: a sampling-line leak or kink can flatten the trace without a real event (false crisis), low-pulmonary-flow states give a falsely low reading, and over-trust in one channel is its own fixation risk. The doctrine's strength (one tiebreaker) becomes a weakness if that tiebreaker itself fails silently.

## The 'macro' / glanceable summary and number-vs-waveform-vs-trend layering — matching the FORM of information to how much attention is available.

**Term of art:** Trend display; Anesthesia Information Management System (AIMS) / automated record; glanceable summary; push (alarm) vs pull (trend) information

Anesthesia displays deliberately layer the SAME variable at three depths so the clinician can engage at the level their current attention budget allows: (1) the big NUMBER for a sub-second glance, (2) the real-time WAVEFORM for shape/quality when there's a moment, and (3) the TREND graph (minutes-to-hours) for trajectory and the slow drift no instantaneous value reveals. Trends are how the field surfaces SLOW derangements that never trip an alarm — a blood pressure declining 3 mmHg every five minutes, a creeping temperature, a slowly rising airway pressure. The record itself (the anesthesia information management system, AIMS) auto-charts these trends so the human's memory is offloaded and the SHAPE of the case over time becomes visible. The design principle: urgent+fast information is pushed (alarms, sound); slow+contextual information is made available at a glance but not forced, so it doesn't consume attention until the human chooses to spend it.

**Failure mode:** Slow drifts are exactly what humans habituate to (boiling-frog) — a trend gentle enough not to alarm is also gentle enough not to be noticed on glance. Auto-charting can create false confidence that 'the record is watching,' and AIMS artifact (a dislodged probe charting a fake value) gets recorded as truth. Trends also compete for the same screen real estate as live data, so under load they're the first thing ignored.

## Vigilance decrement and workload-aware design — the field's empirical knowledge that human monitoring degrades over time and the countermeasures built around it.

**Term of art:** Vigilance decrement; sustained attention; production pressure; handover/handoff (e.g., I-PASS-style); the 'maintenance phase' lull; emergence/induction as high-risk transitions

Anesthesiology directly absorbed the human-factors literature on the VIGILANCE DECREMENT: sustained-attention performance on rare-signal monitoring tasks measurably decays within ~20-30 minutes, and the long, uneventful 'maintenance' phase of an anesthetic is precisely such a task. The field knows the dangerous phases are not only the busy ones but the BORING ones (the long stable case, the overnight case, the post-induction lull) and the TRANSITIONS (induction and emergence are highest-risk). Countermeasures are organizational and design-level: relief/break protocols with structured HANDOVERS (because a fresh operator resets the decrement, at the cost of a handoff-information gap), keeping the human in the loop with active tasks rather than pure passive watching, and matching staffing/automation to workload phase. The deep insight: a human cannot be a reliable continuous SENSOR over hours — so the system must assume detection will lapse and provide a redundant, machine-driven safety net for the rare signal.

**Failure mode:** Handovers are themselves a leading source of error — the relieving clinician inherits an incomplete mental model and misses a slow trend the original operator had internalized ('I would have caught it, but I just took over'). And the cultural fix (just stay vigilant, blame lapses on professionalism) directly contradicts the proven physiology of the decrement, so individuals get blamed for an inevitability the system failed to design around.

## What this field knows
Anesthesiology is the discipline that institutionalized the management of attention as the central safety task. The anesthesiologist sits in front of more continuous data streams than any human can consciously track (ECG, SpO2, EtCO2, BP, agent concentration, ventilator pressures, temperature, BIS, train-of-four, fluid status, surgical field, the conversation in the room) and must keep a patient alive across hours where 99% of the data is normal and the 1% that is not can kill in 90 seconds. The field's literal motto — adopted by the American Society of Anesthesiologists — is "Vigilance." Over decades it has built a deep, hard-won doctrine about WHICH derangement deserves a glance, HOW that signal should be encoded so it reaches a saturated human at the right intensity, and HOW an alarm should map to ONE corrective act rather than a wall of noise. Crucially, the field learned most of this from its failures: the original anesthesia closed-malpractice-claims project found that the deadliest events were not exotic — they were undetected, ordinary derangements (esophageal intubation, disconnects, hypoventilation) that the human did not notice in time. The field's response was not "try harder" but to re-engineer the perception-to-action loop: mandatory continuous monitoring (pulse oximetry, capnography as standards of care), monitors that sonify the patient so vigilance can run in the ears while the eyes are elsewhere, alarm philosophies that grade urgency and demote noise, cockpit-style checklists and challenge-response protocols, crisis manuals that convert a recognized state into a scripted joint procedure, and a sharp distinction between closed-loop control the machine may own (a drug infusion chasing a setpoint) and decisions the human must own (when to declare a crisis, when to call for help, when to abandon the anesthetic plan). What anesthesiology uniquely knows is the design of vigilance under saturation: how to keep a human meaningfully in command of a system that perceives more than they do, when most signals are noise, the cost of a missed real signal is death, and the human's attention — not the data — is the binding constraint.
