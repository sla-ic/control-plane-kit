# Industrial process control / SCADA and alarm management
> Mined via open-learning re-run (Sonnet).

## Alarm Rationalization
**Term of art:** Alarm rationalization (ISA-18.2 standard)
Each alarm in a SCADA system must be justified against a documented consequence: what condition does it detect, what operator action is required, and what is the time-to-respond before harm occurs. A rationalization table (sometimes called the Master Alarm Database) records for every alarm its setpoint, priority tier, required response procedure, and the consequence if the operator takes no action. Alarms without a required operator action are candidates for suppression or reclassification as informational events. The discipline forces the system designer to distinguish signals that demand human judgment from signals that are merely data.
**Failure mode:** Without rationalization, alarms proliferate until operators receive hundreds per hour — "alarm flood" — and begin ignoring them wholesale. The Texas City BP refinery disaster (2005) was partly attributed to operators working in a standing alarm flood state where actionable alarms were indistinguishable from nuisance alarms.

## Alarm Shelving and Suppression
**Term of art:** Alarm shelving / out-of-service suppression
When a sensor is known to be faulty, a unit is deliberately taken offline, or a process is in a transient state (startup, shutdown), an operator or engineer can formally "shelve" an alarm for a defined time window or condition. The shelved alarm is logged with a reason code, an owner, and an automatic reinstatement trigger — it does not permanently disappear, it is suspended with a paper trail. This prevents a known-irrelevant alarm from masking real ones during a constrained period without silencing it forever.
**Failure mode:** Shelved alarms that are never reinstated become permanently invisible. The failure mode is "alarm graveyard" — alarms shelved during a maintenance window years ago that no one ever restored, leaving real process deviations undetected. Good practice requires periodic audits of all shelved alarms.

## Operator Rounds and the Logbook Handover
**Term of art:** Shift handover / operator logbook
At every shift change, the outgoing operator documents the current process state, any abnormal conditions, alarms that are currently active or suppressed, and outstanding work orders in a structured logbook (increasingly a digital form). The incoming operator countersigns this record. This ritual creates a synchronization point where the human's mental model of the plant state is explicitly rebuilt from the written record rather than assumed from memory. The logbook is not a summary — it records specific valve positions, setpoints, and pending actions.
**Failure mode:** Verbal-only handovers (the "walk-and-talk") allow critical context to evaporate. When the next operator doesn't know that a pressure relief valve was manually bypassed two hours ago, they may interpret a rising pressure reading as a sensor fault rather than an actual hazard. Logbook discipline exists precisely to close this gap.

## Deadband and Hysteresis Setpoints
**Term of art:** Alarm deadband / hysteresis
A single threshold alarm — "alert when temperature exceeds 200°C" — will chatter repeatedly if the process variable oscillates around 200. Deadband (also called hysteresis) requires the process variable to retreat a defined distance below the threshold before the alarm can re-trigger. An alarm set at 200°C with a 5°C deadband clears only when temperature drops to 195°C. This prevents the same alarm from firing dozens of times in a short window, each instance demanding operator attention for what is effectively one event.
**Failure mode:** A deadband set too wide can delay the re-triggering of an alarm after a genuine recovery and re-exceedance. The mechanism guards against "chattering alarms" that exhaust operator attention on noise, but misconfigured deadbands can create blind spots after the first alarm acknowledgment.

## Alarm Priority Tiering and the Hierarchy of Consequence
**Term of art:** Priority tiers (ISA-18.2 defines Critical / High / Medium / Low)
Alarms are classified into a small number of priority levels based on time-to-respond and severity of consequence if not acted upon. A Critical alarm demands operator action within seconds and often signals an imminent safety shutdown. A Low alarm may indicate a degraded condition that warrants attention within the next hour. The priority level is not about how loud the beep is — it determines the response procedure, the escalation path, and whether the alarm appears in a dedicated high-priority summary display versus a general list. The ratio of Critical to High to Medium is intended to be heavily bottom-weighted so that the Critical queue remains sparse and always actionable.
**Failure mode:** Priority inflation: over time, engineers classify ambiguous alarms as High "just to be safe," collapsing the meaningful distinction between tiers. When 40% of alarms are High priority, the operator's triage heuristic breaks down entirely, and the priority system becomes cosmetic.

## First-Out Annunciation
**Term of art:** First-out / first-up alarm
In a cascade failure — where one process upset triggers a chain of downstream alarms — the system records and highlights which alarm fired first, before the operator acknowledged anything. This is the diagnostic root: the pump that lost suction before the downstream pressure alarms, the temperature sensor that spiked before the interlock tripped the furnace. First-out logic is wired into the control system at the hardware or logic-solver level, not inferred after the fact. The operator's primary task in a shutdown event is to find the first-out alarm and treat that as the cause, not any of the subsequent effects.
**Failure mode:** If the first-out alarm is itself a nuisance alarm that operators routinely acknowledge without acting — because it chatters constantly — the causal chain becomes ambiguous. The system knew the root cause; the interface had trained operators to dismiss it.

## Safety Instrumented System Separation
**Term of art:** SIS / SIL (Safety Integrity Level) independence
In high-consequence plants (refineries, chemical plants, nuclear), the safety shutdown system is physically and logically separate from the SCADA/DCS that operators interact with. The Basic Process Control System (BPCS) is what operators tune and monitor. The Safety Instrumented System (SIS) operates on its own sensors, logic solvers, and final elements (valves). The SIS acts automatically when a Safety Instrumented Function (SIF) threshold is crossed — it does not wait for operator action. The SCADA interface shows the operator what the SIS has done after it acts, not a prompt to approve it. This separates the human-in-the-loop (process optimization) from the system-in-the-loop (safety enforcement).
**Failure mode:** Operators who override or bypass SIS interlocks to avoid nuisance trips — a practice called "defeat" or "defeat of safety" — collapse this architectural separation. Each defeat is supposed to require written authorization; without that discipline, the independence guarantee dissolves silently.

## What this field knows
Industrial process control understands that a human paired with a perceptually-superior system is not the primary actor — they are the judgment layer inserted between continuous automated perception and consequential physical action. The field's core insight is that attention is finite and must be metered: the system earns the right to interrupt a human only when a required action exists, a time window constrains it, and a specific consequence is documented. The field also knows that the interface between human and system degrades over time through normalization — alarms that should trigger response become background noise through repetition — and that counteracting this requires periodic, structured audits that force re-rationalization of every signal. Finally, the shift handover ritual reveals a deep understanding that the human's mental model of system state is not continuous but reconstructed at intervals, and that the quality of reconstruction determines whether the next operator acts on reality or on inherited assumption.
