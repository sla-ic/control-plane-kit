# Wildland fire lookout and dispatch (Osborne firefinder)
> Mined via open-learning re-run (Sonnet).

## Azimuth triangulation via the Osborne firefinder
**Term of art:** Firefinder (also: alidade, crosshairs on topographic map disk)
The Osborne firefinder is a rotating sighting device mounted at the center of a large topographic map disk inside a lookout tower. When a lookout spots smoke, they rotate the alidade until the sight line bisects the smoke column, then read an azimuth bearing directly off the map disk. That bearing is radioed to dispatch, who plots it on a central map. A single bearing is a line, not a location — a second lookout station sighting the same smoke from a different angle produces a second line; the intersection ("crossed azimuths") gives a grid coordinate. The human only calls out one number (an azimuth), and the spatial reasoning is distributed across multiple stations and the dispatcher's map table.
**Failure mode:** Smoke drift and atmospheric refraction cause parallax error; a single-lookout report that cannot be triangulated produces only a "line of probability," delaying initial attack. If two stations triangulate on different smoke columns believing them the same fire, dispatch gets a false fix.

## The lookout's log and hourly weather observation
**Term of art:** Observer's log, spot weather request
Every staffed lookout station maintains a handwritten log recording each observation: time, azimuth, estimated distance, smoke color (white = damp fuel, gray/black = heavy fuel), and any change in behavior. At scheduled intervals (typically every hour during fire weather), the lookout reads a set of weather instruments — dry-bulb/wet-bulb thermometer, wind speed and direction, relative humidity — and transmits a standardized weather observation to dispatch and to the National Weather Service forecaster who can issue a "spot weather forecast" for a specific fire. The ritual forces structured attention: the lookout must read and record even when nothing is burning, maintaining calibration.
**Failure mode:** Lookouts who skip routine observations during quiet periods lose situational calibration; when fire does appear, their weather data is stale and the spot forecast is degraded. The failure mode is "attention drift in the absence of signal."

## Fire behavior description protocol (smoke column report)
**Term of art:** Initial attack report, size-up narrative
A trained lookout reports smoke not as a bare sighting but as a structured behavior description: smoke column height, color, whether the column is building or collapsing, whether spotting (embers lofting ahead of the fire front) is visible, and the terrain aspect below the column. This is an interpretive act, not a raw percept — the lookout is encoding fire behavior, not just location. Dispatch uses this description to infer rate of spread, fuel type, and initial attack resource requirements before any ground crew arrives. The column description is an inference about the system state encoded in human-readable form.
**Failure mode:** An inexperienced lookout who reports only "smoke visible" strips the actionable content; dispatch receives a signal without behavior, and initial attack resource sizing defaults to the minimum, sometimes under-committing to a fast-moving fire.

## Radio discipline and the single-frequency net
**Term of art:** Net control, lookout-to-dispatch protocol, radio net
All lookout stations and dispatch share a single radio frequency during fire season. Net control (typically the dispatch center) arbitrates who can transmit. Lookouts do not interrupt each other; they wait for a clear channel or key the radio twice to signal urgency. Messages follow a structured format: station identification, then the report content, then "over." Acknowledgment is mandatory — the lookout does not consider a report delivered until dispatch reads it back. This single-channel discipline creates a shared audio common operating picture: every station on the net hears every report, and experienced lookouts cross-check each other's sightings in real time without additional coordination overhead.
**Failure mode:** Simultaneous transmissions cause "stepping on" — both signals are lost. In high-fire-weather periods with multiple smokes, net discipline collapses if stations transmit without waiting, and dispatch loses track of which reports have been acknowledged. The protocol guards against the assumption that transmission equals delivery.

## The dispatch map table and resource status board
**Term of art:** Dispatch log, resource status board (ICS Form 215 precursor), tactical map
The dispatch center maintains a physical map (later whiteboard, later CAD) on which every active fire's location is plotted, every resource's current assignment is tracked, and every lookout report is marked. Resource status — engines, crews, aircraft — is maintained on a separate board showing "available," "en route," "committed," and "out of service." The dispatcher's job is to hold the state of the whole system in one place and present it to the Incident Commander as a pre-synthesized picture. The human decision-maker (Acme) does not reconstruct system state from raw data; they receive a curated snapshot and issue orders against it.
**Failure mode:** When the dispatch map diverges from actual resource positions — a crew that moved without calling in, an aircraft that diverted — the Acme's orders are based on phantom state. The failure is "map drift": the model of the world at dispatch and the world itself decouple silently.

## Lookout placement and the "seen area" map
**Term of art:** Seen area, coverage map, lookout siting study
Before a lookout tower is staffed, land managers produce a "seen area" map: a topographic analysis showing exactly which terrain is visible from that tower's elevation and which terrain is in shadow (dead ground). Lookouts are trained to know their own blind spots and to annotate reports with "area not observable from this station." Coverage gaps are filled either by additional towers, aerial patrol, or remote sensors. The system explicitly models its own perceptual limits and routes around them — the lookout's job description includes knowing what they cannot see.
**Failure mode:** A lookout who does not know their dead ground confidently reports "no smoke visible" in a zone they cannot actually observe, creating a false negative that delays detection of an ignition in a blind corridor. The failure mode is perceptual overconfidence — mistaking absence of signal for absence of fire.

## Initial attack dispatch authority and pre-authorized response
**Term of art:** Dispatch authority, pre-attack plan, initial attack (IA) matrix
Before fire season, each ranger district or forest prepares a pre-attack plan: a decision matrix specifying which resources are automatically dispatched based on fire weather index, time of day, fuel type, and location. When a lookout report meets defined thresholds, dispatch does not wait for human approval — the matrix authorizes the initial resource dispatch immediately. The Acme arrives to a fire that already has resources en route. The system converts slow deliberative decision-making into fast pre-committed action for the highest-frequency case, reserving human judgment for escalation and exceptions.
**Failure mode:** A pre-attack matrix calibrated to average conditions will under-respond to an outlier fire weather day and over-respond on routine days, wasting resources. The failure is that pre-committed logic cannot adapt to conditions outside its design envelope without human override — and the override mechanism (calling the supervisor) takes time the matrix was designed to eliminate.

## What this field knows
Wildland fire lookout and dispatch uniquely understands that a perceptually-superior system (the network of lookouts and sensors) is only as useful as the translation layer that converts raw percepts into actionable behavior descriptions — a bare azimuth or a bare "smoke visible" does not close a loop. The field also understands that the human decision-maker must be shielded from raw system state and instead receive synthesized snapshots (the dispatch map, the resource board) so that judgment can be applied at the right altitude. Perhaps most distinctively, this domain explicitly maps and announces its own blind spots: the "seen area" discipline treats perceptual gaps as first-class system facts, not as embarrassments to be hidden. The result is a joint human-system architecture where the system's job is to pre-compress signal into decision-ready form, and the human's job is to apply judgment to the residue the system cannot resolve alone.
