## Place every human-machine interaction on an explicit Level of Automation (LOA) scale, and recognize the interface's real job is to pick the right rung — for each sub-function separately, not for the whole system.

**Term of art:** Levels of Automation (LOA); Parasuraman-Sheridan-Wickens four-stage model; 'management-by-consent' (act only on approval) vs 'management-by-exception' (act unless vetoed)

Sheridan & Verplank's 10-level scale runs from (1) human does everything, through (4) computer suggests one option and human approves, (6) computer acts unless human vetoes within a time window, (8) computer acts and tells human only if asked, to (10) computer acts and ignores the human. The pivotal design move is that the LOA is not fixed per system — Parasuraman/Sheridan/Wickens' four-stage model says you set a DIFFERENT level for each of four information-processing stages: (1) information acquisition, (2) information analysis, (3) decision selection, (4) action implementation. So an interface can perceive and analyze at a near-autonomous level (the system reads every channel) while deliberately holding decision-selection at level 4-5 (recommend-and-wait) for the human. The choice of rung per stage IS the answer to 'what rises and for what joint action.'

**Failure mode:** Picking one global LOA for a whole system forces a bad compromise: high enough to offload work but high enough to push the human out of the loop. The known trap is choosing a high level for action while leaving the human responsible for a decision they no longer have the situational basis to make.

## Design against the 'Ironies of Automation' as first principles: assume that automating the easy parts makes the remaining human job HARDER and the human a worse monitor — then engineer the interface to counteract that, not to celebrate the automation.

**Term of art:** Ironies of Automation (Bainbridge 1983); the 'out-of-the-loop' (OOTL) performance problem; skill atrophy / manual-skill decay

Lisanne Bainbridge's 1983 'Ironies of Automation' is the field's load-bearing text. The ironies: (a) the designer who automates because they distrust the human leaves the human the tasks the designer couldn't figure out how to automate — the hardest residue; (b) a human asked to monitor an automated process that rarely fails cannot sustain vigilance (it is physiologically near-impossible to watch a reliable process for rare deviations); (c) a human kept out of the control loop loses the manual skill and the live mental model needed to take over in the exact emergency where takeover is demanded. The design consequence is concrete: do NOT build an interface that asks the human to passively watch and occasionally rubber-stamp. Build one that keeps the human's hands and judgment engaged at a tempo that preserves their model — e.g., periodically requiring active input, narrating the system's reasoning so the human stays current, or deliberately leaving some sub-tasks manual to keep skills warm.

**Failure mode:** The 'automation conundrum': the more reliable and autonomous the system, the less the human monitors it, the worse their situation awareness, and the more catastrophic and surprising the eventual handback. AF447: pilots handed a degraded aircraft they no longer had the model to fly. The interface that perceives the most can produce the least-capable human at the decisive moment.

## Treat Situation Awareness (SA) as the quantity the interface exists to protect, and design to the THREE levels of SA rather than to data completeness.

**Term of art:** Situation Awareness (Endsley's 3 levels); SAGAT (Situation Awareness Global Assessment Technique); SA-Oriented Design; 'SA demons'

Mica Endsley's model defines SA as three levels: Level 1 perception of elements, Level 2 comprehension of what they mean together, Level 3 projection of what they will do next. The interface's job is not to deliver Level-1 data (which a perceiving system has in overwhelming abundance) but to build the human's Level 2 and Level 3 — meaning and projection — because those are what let a human act and take over. Endsley's SA-Oriented Design and the SAGAT measurement technique (freeze the simulation, blank the displays, query the operator on what they believed was true) operationalize this: you measure whether the human actually HELD the right model, not whether the data was on screen. A correlate: 'SA demons' — named ways interfaces destroy SA: attentional narrowing, requisite memory traps, data overload, misplaced salience, complexity creep, errant mental models, the out-of-the-loop syndrome.

**Failure mode:** Data overload and misplaced salience: an interface that surfaces everything (Level 1) while building no comprehension actively degrades SA — the human has the data but not the picture. Measuring 'was it displayed?' instead of 'did they comprehend it?' hides the failure until the takeover moment.

## Engineer notifications via Adaptive / Adaptable Automation — let WHAT rises and at WHAT level shift dynamically based on operator state, workload, and stakes, rather than a fixed surfacing policy.

**Term of art:** Adaptive automation vs adaptable automation; Dynamic Function Allocation; critical-event / workload-based / physiological invocation logic; DTAP (Dynamic Task Allocation)

The field distinguishes 'adaptive' automation (the system changes the LOA based on triggers) from 'adaptable' automation (the human chooses when to delegate). Triggers studied and used: critical-event logic (raise autonomy/alerting when a defined hazardous event occurs), operator-workload measurement (offload or quiet down when the human is saturated; re-engage them when idle to fight complacency), performance-based (hand back when the human's performance degrades), and physiological triggers (eye-tracking, EEG, heart-rate variability to detect overload or disengagement). The interface thus modulates its own intrusiveness: it pushes hard and takes more control when the human is overloaded or a critical event fires, and deliberately pulls the human BACK IN during lulls to keep them in the loop. This is the field's direct answer to 'in what form / at what tempo.'

**Failure mode:** Adaptive systems can themselves cause automation surprise — if the system changes its own level without the human understanding why or when, the human loses track of who is in control (a meta-mode-confusion). Poorly tuned triggers also cause thrashing: handing control back and forth at the worst tempo.

## Make the automation's MODE and INTENT continuously and saliently observable — the field's antidote to mode confusion is not less automation but feedback that answers 'what is it doing, why, and what will it do next?'

**Term of art:** Mode confusion; automation surprise (Sarter & Woods); observability vs data availability; directability; 'strong, silent automation' (the anti-pattern); the automation as 'team player'

Mode confusion / 'automation surprise' (Sarter & Woods' core finding from glass-cockpit research) happens when the human's model of which mode the automation is in diverges from reality — the system does something the human doesn't expect because it's in a mode they didn't know about. The documented fix is 'observability' (not mere 'data availability'): feedback that is salient, future-oriented, and pattern-based, showing not just current state but transitions and intent. Concrete instruments: mode annunciators, FMA (Flight Mode Annunciator) lines, energy/trend displays that show where the system is heading, and 'directability' — the human can always interrogate and redirect. Woods frames the goal as the automation being a good 'team player': observable, directable, and able to signal when it is reaching the edge of its competence.

**Failure mode:** 'Strong but silent' automation: highly capable, low feedback. It acts competently right up until it reaches the edge of its envelope, then fails or hands back with no warning and no explanation — the human is surprised precisely because the system worked so well it gave no reason to watch.

## Calibrate TRUST explicitly as a design variable — the interface must reveal the automation's competence boundaries so the human's reliance tracks actual reliability (neither over- nor under-trust).

**Term of art:** Trust calibration; appropriate reliance; over-trust (complacency) vs disuse (under-trust); resolution & specificity of trust; the 'lumberjack effect'; purpose/process/performance bases of trust

Lee & See's 'Trust in Automation: Designing for Appropriate Reliance' frames the goal as calibration: trust should match the automation's true capability, with good 'resolution' (the human can tell WHICH situations it's good at) and 'specificity' (trust is sub-function-specific, not a global feeling). The interface earns calibrated trust by exposing the basis of its actions — its purpose, process, and performance (the three bases of trust). Practically: show confidence/uncertainty, show WHY a recommendation was made, and surface known failure regions so the human under-relies exactly where the system is weak. The 'lumberjack effect' (Onnasch et al.) quantifies the stakes: as LOA rises, routine performance and workload improve, but the COST of an automation failure grows and SA drops — a steep cliff. Calibration is how you let the human stand back from that cliff.

**Failure mode:** Automation bias and complacency: an over-trusted system gets the human to accept wrong recommendations (commission errors) or miss events it should have caught (omission errors). The Patriot fratricides and aviation incidents trace to operators deferring to a confident automated call. Conversely, an uncalibrated system that cries wolf produces disuse — the human turns it off or ignores it.

## Run notifications through proven ALERT/ALARM doctrine that is built to fight false-alarm fatigue: prioritize, suppress, and stage alerts so the rare true signal actually penetrates.

**Term of art:** Alarm management (EEMUA 191, ISA 18.2); alarm rationalization; alarm flood; shelving/suppression; state-based alarming; 'cry wolf' effect; Signal Detection Theory (criterion vs sensitivity); advisory/caution/warning tiers

Process-control and aviation alarm management (e.g., the EEMUA 191 / ISA 18.2 alarm-management standards, and the 'cry wolf' literature) treat alert design as a discipline with hard rules: an alarm must be actionable (if there's nothing the human can do, it's not an alarm), it must be prioritized by urgency AND consequence, and the total alarm RATE must stay within human cognitive limits (standards specify target alarms-per-hour ceilings). Techniques: alarm rationalization (justify every alarm's existence), shelving/suppression of known nuisance alarms, alarm flood suppression during upset conditions, state-based alarming (suppress alarms irrelevant to the current operating mode), and escalation tiers. The Theory of Signal Detection (SDT) underpins this: every threshold trades hits against false alarms, and you must set the criterion deliberately given the base rate and the cost of each error type. The cockpit's layered scheme — advisory / caution / warning, each with distinct color, tone, and required response — is the canonical staged form.

**Failure mode:** Alarm flood and false-alarm desensitization: in upset conditions the human is buried in hundreds of simultaneous alarms (Three Mile Island, Texaco Milford Haven) and the meaningful ones are lost; chronic false alarms train the operator to ignore or disable the system. An interface that perceives everything will, by default, generate exactly this flood unless rationalized.

## Design Decision Support to keep the human in DECISION authority by presenting options-with-rationale rather than a single answer — and structure HOW a recommendation is framed to avoid anchoring.

**Term of art:** Decision support vs decision automation; recommend-and-consent; anchoring/automation bias in advice; Rasmussen's decision ladder; Cognitive Work Analysis (CWA)

The supervisory-control stance is that for the decision-selection stage you usually want a mid-level LOA: the system narrows the field and explains the tradeoffs, the human chooses. Decision-support practice surfaces the alternatives, the evidence and assumptions behind each, and the uncertainty — so the human reasons WITH the system rather than ratifying it. The framing matters: presenting a single confident recommendation first produces anchoring/automation bias; presenting a small comparable set with rationale preserves the human's independent judgment. A related instrument is the 'decision ladder' (Rasmussen) from Cognitive Work Analysis, which maps the full reasoning path from observation to action so a designer can choose exactly which rungs the system pre-computes and which it leaves to the human — i.e., where to inject support without short-circuiting human cognition.

**Failure mode:** Single-recommendation decision aids cause the human to stop generating their own hypotheses (cognitive offloading of the wrong stage). When the aid is wrong, the anchored human rarely recovers the alternative it suppressed — the support tool quietly became the decision-maker.

## Use Ecological Interface Design (EID): represent the work domain's constraints and goal-relations directly so the human perceives the situation's MEANING at a glance, instead of reading raw variables.

**Term of art:** Ecological Interface Design (EID); Abstraction Hierarchy; Skill-Rule-Knowledge (SRK) framework; emergent features / configural displays; direct perception

Rasmussen & Vicente's EID builds the display from an Abstraction Hierarchy (the work domain modeled from physical components up through functions to overall purposes) and targets all three of Rasmussen's levels of cognitive control: Skill-based (direct perception-action), Rule-based (recognize a situation, apply a rule), and Knowledge-based (reason from first principles when novel). EID makes higher-order properties — like whether a system is within safe limits, or the balance of competing goals — visually emergent (e.g., a polygon that stays symmetric when balanced and distorts when not), so the human offloads routine monitoring to fast perception and reserves effortful knowledge-based reasoning for genuinely novel events. This directly answers 'in what form': show constraints and relationships, not numbers; make the abnormal pop perceptually.

**Failure mode:** Single-sensor-single-indicator displays (one number per variable) force the human into slow knowledge-based reasoning to integrate them — fine in calm conditions, catastrophic under time pressure when the human can't assemble the picture fast enough. The meaning is present in the data but not in a perceptible form.

## Reject the 'substitution myth' — design function allocation by what makes the HUMAN-MACHINE TEAM resilient, not by a Fitts-list tally of who's better at what (the MABA-MABA trap).

**Term of art:** Substitution myth; Fitts List / MABA-MABA; 'function allocation transforms practice'; coordination cost; common ground; Ten Challenges for Making Automation a Team Player (Klein/Woods/Bradshaw et al.); the 'envisioned world problem'

The original Fitts List (1951, 'Men Are Better At / Machines Are Better At') tabulates human vs machine strengths to divide labor. The field's mature critique (Dekker & Woods, 'MABA-MABA or Abracadabra') is that allocation is NOT zero-sum substitution: automating a function transforms the whole task, creating new coordination demands, new monitoring burdens, and new failure modes ('every automation is a new team member you now have to coordinate with'). So you don't ask 'who is better at X' and hand X over; you ask 'how does the joint system as a whole stay coordinated and resilient when things go wrong.' This reframes the interface from a divider of labor to a coordination surface that maintains common ground between human and machine. Klein, Woods et al.'s 'Ten Challenges for Making Automation a Team Player' codifies the requirements: the agent must be predictable, directable, able to maintain common ground, signal its status and intentions, and be observable.

**Failure mode:** Allocating by substitution creates 'clumsy automation' — it reduces workload when load is already low (calm cruise) and increases it exactly when load is already high (the emergency it can't handle hands everything back). The team was never designed; two agents end up with no shared picture at the worst moment.

## Make takeover/handoff a first-class, time-budgeted, choreographed transition — the moment control passes between machine and human is a designed event with a 'transition time,' not an instantaneous flip.

**Term of art:** Handover / handback; takeover request (TOR) and takeover time/budget; human-in-the-loop vs human-on-the-loop vs human-out-of-the-loop; graceful degradation; transfer-of-authority; 'fly-by-wire envelope protection' as a soft-handoff form

Supervisory control and (latterly) automated-driving research treat the handback as the highest-risk moment and design it explicitly: the system must signal it is approaching its competence boundary BEFORE it fails, provide a 'takeover request' with enough lead time for the human to rebuild situation awareness (research quantifies takeover time — the seconds a disengaged human needs to regain control safely), and stage the transfer rather than dumping a degraded situation on a cold human. The 'human-on-the-loop' (supervising, can intervene) vs 'human-in-the-loop' (must approve each action) vs 'human-out-of-the-loop' distinction sets who can even catch the handoff. Good practice keeps the human warm enough (via the in-the-loop tempo above) that the takeover is feasible at all, and uses graceful degradation so the system fails into a safe, simplified state the human can grasp.

**Failure mode:** The 'cold handoff': automation works until it abruptly quits and hands a startled, out-of-the-loop human a degraded situation with no lead time and no context (AF447's autopilot disconnect; many Level-2/3 driving disengagements). The transition itself becomes the accident. The fix is not better hardware but a budgeted, pre-announced, context-restoring handover.

## Maintain a shared, inspectable representation of intent and state — 'common ground' — as the durable artifact the joint cognitive system references, so human and machine never silently diverge on what's true or what the goal is.

**Term of art:** Joint Cognitive Systems (Hollnagel & Woods); common ground & common-ground breakdown; coordination/'choreography' of joint activity; directability; basic compact (the agreement to coordinate); 'represent the problem, not just the data'

Hollnagel & Woods' 'Joint Cognitive Systems' reframes the unit of analysis from human-plus-tool to a single cognitive system that must stay coordinated. The mechanism is common ground (Klein/Clark): a continuously maintained, mutually inspectable model of the situation, the plan, role assignments, and each party's status. Practically the interface maintains and exposes: the system's current goal/plan, its assumptions, what it has done and why, what it's uncertain about, and where it believes the human stands — and lets the human correct any of these (directability). When common ground breaks ('fundamental common ground breakdown'), coordination collapses even if both parties are individually competent. The design implication: the interface's core deliverable is not a feed of events but a living, editable shared model that both parties write to and read from, enabling them to close loops together rather than view status.

**Failure mode:** Silent divergence of mental models: the human believes the system is pursuing goal A while it pursues B, or believes the system has handled something it hasn't (a common-ground breakdown). Because nothing forces the models to reconcile, the gap is invisible until an action based on the wrong shared picture goes wrong — the defining failure of a system that perceives more than the human but never reconciles what each side believes.

## What this field knows
This field is the literal academic frame for one human supervising and acting through an autonomous perceiver. Its central, hard-won insight is counterintuitive for interface design: better automation does NOT mean showing the human more or asking them less. The field has documented, with body counts (Air France 447, Three Mile Island, USS Vincennes, Patriot fratricides, Uberself-driving fatality), that the act of removing the human from the moment-to-moment loop is exactly what destroys their ability to act well when it matters — the "automation conundrum" / "ironies of automation." So the field does not treat the interface as a window for viewing status; it treats it as the mechanism that allocates COGNITIVE FUNCTION (who senses, who decides, who acts, who monitors) and that keeps the human's internal model of the situation aligned with reality. The deep knowledge here is about: (1) the human is a terrible passive monitor of a reliable automated process and a worse one as the automation gets better, so attention must be ENGINEERED, not assumed; (2) what rises and in what form is a function-allocation decision (a "level of automation"), not a display decision, and it can and should shift dynamically with workload, stakes, and trust; (3) the failure modes are specific and named (mode confusion, automation surprise, complacency/bias, out-of-the-loop unfamiliarity, clumsy automation, the lumberjack effect, the substitution myth) and each one maps to a design constraint; (4) the goal artifact is a shared, inspectable representation of intent and situation (common ground / a "joint cognitive system") so human and machine can reference the same world and hand control back and forth gracefully. The unique thing this field offers a designer is a vocabulary and a set of instruments for deciding NOT to surface things, for surfacing them at the right tempo, and for keeping a human directable-and-accountable rather than a rubber stamp or a startled fallback.
