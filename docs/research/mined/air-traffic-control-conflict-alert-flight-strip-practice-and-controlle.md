## The 'picture' (or 'having the picture' / 'losing the picture') — the controller's continuously-maintained mental model of the entire traffic situation as a dynamic, projected-forward whole, not a snapshot. Controllers are trained that their primary job is to *hold the picture*, and the cardinal failure is 'losing the picture' (situational awareness collapse), at which point doctrine says to stop taking new traffic, hand off, and rebuild rather than push through.

**Term of art:** 'The picture' / 'situational awareness' / 'losing the picture'

The display and tools exist to FEED and PROTECT a mental model that lives in the human, not to BE the model. Information is filtered, decluttered, and paced specifically so the picture stays coherent. This is the field's foundational inversion: the scarce resource is the human's coherent internal model, and every design choice serves its integrity. The transfer: an org-perception interface should be judged by whether it keeps the human's *mental model of the org* coherent and current — not by how much it displays. 'Losing the picture' is a real, nameable, recoverable state with a prescribed response (shed load, don't power through).

**Failure mode:** Task saturation / overload: too many simultaneous demands and the picture fragments. Once lost, adding MORE information makes it worse, not better — the recovery is to REDUCE inflow (stop accepting handoffs), not increase it. A dashboard that floods a saturated human is actively harmful.

## Tiered conflict alerting by time-to-act: the system runs distinct layers — MTCD (Medium-Term Conflict Detection, ~3–20+ min lookahead, advisory, 'you have time to plan'), STCA (Short-Term Conflict Alert, ~<2 min, urgent, 'resolve now'), and at the last-ditch airborne layer TCAS/ACAS with its TA (Traffic Advisory, 'watch') then RA (Resolution Advisory, 'climb/descend NOW, comply immediately'). Each tier has a different sensory signature, urgency, and required response.

**Term of art:** STCA (Short-Term Conflict Alert), MTCD (Medium-Term Conflict Detection), TCAS/ACAS RA vs TA, 'safety net'

The machine perceives ALL projected conflicts but deliberately sorts them by how much thinking-time the human has. Advisory tiers invite planning and can be reasoned with or overridden; the imperative tier (STCA flashing, RA aural) demands immediate, near-scripted compliance and is intentionally rare. Crucially the tiers are kept SEPARATE so the rare 'act now' alarm is never buried among 'maybe later' notices. Transfer: stratify what rises by the human's available decision-latency, and never let advisory-volume contaminate the imperative channel.

**Failure mode:** If thresholds are mistuned, the urgent tier fires on non-events and controllers learn to discount it ('crying wolf'). Conversely, the famous Überlingen (2002) disaster: the TCAS RA told the pilots to climb but the controller verbally told them to descend — two authorities issued conflicting imperative instructions and the resolution-layer authority was not unambiguous. Lesson the field codified: when the last-ditch automated imperative speaks, it must outrank the human advisory — authority precedence must be settled IN ADVANCE.

## Nuisance-alert suppression as an explicit safety discipline. STCA and TCAS are deliberately tuned, geographically/altitude filtered, and inhibited in known-safe geometries (e.g., parallel approaches, on-the-ground, below certain altitudes) so that an alert that fires MEANS something. The false-alert rate is tracked as a managed safety metric, and a high nuisance rate is treated as a defect to be engineered out.

**Term of art:** 'Nuisance alerts' / 'false alerts' / alert inhibition / 'crying wolf'

The field learned that the value of an alarm is inversely proportional to how often it lies. So enormous engineering effort goes into NOT alerting in situations the machine can perceive but that are operationally fine. The alert budget is treated as sacred and scarce. Transfer: the right to interrupt the human is a rationed, defended resource; every false interruption is a withdrawal from the trust account, and suppression logic (known-safe patterns, context filters) is as important as detection logic.

**Failure mode:** Alert fatigue / desensitization: clinically documented in aviation and medicine alike — when alarms are too frequent or too often false, humans suppress, mute, or stop seeing them, so the one real alarm is missed. The failure is not the human ignoring the alarm; it's the system having spent its credibility.

## Flight progress strips and 'strip passing': each aircraft is represented by a physical (or electronic, EFS) strip showing callsign, type, route, cleared level, and intentions. The strip is the controller's externalized memory and plan. Controllers physically annotate strips (cleared altitudes, headings, handoff status), 'cock' a strip out of alignment in the bay to flag something needing attention, and physically *hand* the strip to the next controller to transfer responsibility.

**Term of art:** Flight progress strip, strip bay, 'cocking' a strip, EFS (Electronic Flight Strips), annotation

The strip externalizes the controller's INTENT and the aircraft's PLAN into a shared, manipulable artifact. The strip bay's physical layout (sequence, offset) encodes priority and status non-verbally. Annotation is the act of committing a decision; cocking a strip is a self-addressed alert; passing the strip is closing a transfer of ownership. The picture lives partly in the human's head and partly in this tangible, glanceable, rearrangeable object. Transfer: give the human an externalized, annotatable representation of intent-and-plan per entity that doubles as working memory, priority encoding, and the medium of handoff — not a read-only status row.

**Failure mode:** The shift from paper to electronic strips repeatedly degraded the subtle non-verbal/tactile cues (the felt 'weight' of a busy bay, peripheral awareness of a colleague cocking a strip, the haptics of handing one over). Designers learned that the artifact carried far more tacit coordination signal than its data fields suggested — digitizing only the data threw away the picture-keeping affordances.

## Read-back / hear-back as the loop-closing protocol. Every clearance (e.g., 'Climb and maintain flight level 350') is read back verbatim by the pilot; the controller must HEAR the read-back and correct any error (hear-back). An instruction is not considered executed until this two-way confirmation completes. Standard phraseology (ICAO) constrains the wording so ambiguity is minimized.

**Term of art:** Read-back / hear-back, standard phraseology, 'readback-hearback error'

A control action is treated as an explicit closed loop with mandatory acknowledgment, not fire-and-forget. The read-back surfaces mishearings; the hear-back catches the controller's own slips. The atomic, scripted phraseology means both parties share an exact, low-entropy command vocabulary. Transfer: agent↔human instructions should be atomic, in a constrained shared vocabulary, and require an explicit acknowledged read-back so the loop is *provably* closed — and the human gets a last chance to catch the agent's (or their own) error before it executes.

**Failure mode:** 'Readback-hearback error' — a recognized incident category where the pilot reads back wrong and the controller fails to catch it, or both drift into expectation bias (hearing the expected level, not the spoken one). Also 'climb via / expect' confusions. The protocol works only if the hear-back step is actively attended, not rubber-stamped.

## Datalink intent exchange (CPDLC) and trajectory-based operations: increasingly the cleared route/level is exchanged as structured DATA between ground automation and the aircraft FMS, not just voice. The controller's intent and the aircraft's intended trajectory become a shared machine-readable object that conflict-detection tools (MTCD) check against. 'Trajectory-Based Operations' makes the agreed 4D trajectory the contract.

**Term of art:** CPDLC (Controller–Pilot Data Link Communications), TBO (Trajectory-Based Operations), 4D trajectory, 'business/mission trajectory'

Intent is made first-class and machine-readable on BOTH sides: the ground knows what the aircraft intends (downlinked trajectory) and the aircraft knows what the ground intends (uplinked clearance), and the conflict-checker reasons over intentions rather than only current positions. This is the deepest version of 'share intent both ways': the machine isn't guessing the human's plan, it's reading it from the same contract the human authored. Transfer: for an agent to usefully check an org for conflicts, the human's INTENT must be captured as structured data the agent reads — predictions over 'what is planned' beat alarms over 'what is currently true.'

**Failure mode:** Mode confusion / automation surprise: when the human's mental model of the agreed trajectory diverges from what the automation actually loaded (e.g., FMS programmed differently than cleared), conflict checks validate the wrong intent. Also latency: datalink is slower than voice for time-critical instructions, so it's reserved for non-urgent intent and voice retains the imperative channel.

## Explicit handoff / point-out ritual for transferring trajectory ownership between controllers/sectors. A precise sequence: initiate handoff, the receiving controller ACCEPTS (electronically and/or verbally), and only then is the aircraft told to switch frequency ('contact Center on 133.4'). 'Point-out' is a lighter ritual: I flag an aircraft that will clip your airspace so you're aware, without transferring control. 'Your control / my control' verbally fixes who owns the decision at any instant.

**Term of art:** Handoff, point-out, 'your control / my control', 'radar contact', frequency change

Ownership of each trajectory is singular, explicit, and transferred only by mutual handshake — there is never a moment where it's ambiguous who is responsible for a given aircraft. The handoff is a closed transaction (offer → accept → frequency change confirmed). 'Point-out' separates 'you need to KNOW about this' from 'you now OWN this.' Transfer: in human-agent and multi-agent settings, ownership of any live thread must be singular and transferred by explicit acknowledged handshake; distinguish FYI-awareness from custody-transfer; never leave 'who acts on this' implicit.

**Failure mode:** Dropped/missed handoff: an aircraft on a frequency nobody is actively working ('NORDO'-adjacent / frequency limbo), or two controllers each believing the other owns it (diffusion of responsibility). The ritual exists precisely because ambiguous ownership at a boundary is where things fall through cracks.

## Display decluttering, data-block management, and selective disclosure. The radar scope shows minimal data tags by default; the controller can expand a data block for detail, and the system declutters automatically under density. Conflict pairs are highlighted (halos, leader-line emphasis, color/blink) to make the relevant relationship pop out of a dense field. Altitude filtering shows only traffic in the controller's slab of airspace.

**Term of art:** Data block / data tag, decluttering, altitude filtering, conflict halo, 'target'

The interface is designed so the RELEVANT relationship (the converging pair) is perceptually salient against a deliberately suppressed background — pre-attentive features (motion/blink/color) are reserved for what matters, and everything else is kept quiet and expandable-on-demand. The human pulls detail when needed rather than being pushed all of it. Transfer: surface the *relationship that needs action* with pre-attentive salience, keep the rest decluttered and queryable on demand, and filter to the human's domain of responsibility — don't render the full firehose at uniform visual weight.

**Failure mode:** Display clutter and 'head-down' tunneling — controllers fixating on the screen and on managing the display itself, losing the broader picture (and, in tower, losing the out-the-window scan). Over-coding with color/blink re-creates the nuisance problem in the visual channel: if everything is salient, nothing is.

## Sectorization, MONITOR/control-position splitting, and dynamic load balancing. Airspace is divided into sectors sized so one controller's traffic stays within human capacity; busy sectors are split into two positions (a Radar/Executive controller who talks to aircraft and a Planner/Coordinator who manages strips, coordinates with adjacent sectors, and looks further ahead). Sectors are combined or de-combined as traffic rises and falls. Flow-management (ground delay programs, miles-in-trail) throttles INPUT before it ever reaches a saturated sector.

**Term of art:** Sector, Radar/Executive vs Planner/Coordinator (D-side), 'splitting the sector', flow management / ground delay program, miles-in-trail

Human bandwidth is a hard, respected constraint: the SYSTEM is reshaped (split the sector, add a planner, throttle the inflow) to keep each human under their cognitive limit, rather than expecting the human to absorb whatever arrives. The exec/planner split explicitly separates 'act on the now' from 'reason about what's coming' — two different cognitive jobs, two roles. Transfer: when load rises, restructure the human-agent system (add a planning layer, split scope, throttle intake upstream) instead of pushing a saturated human harder; separate the 'execute now' attention from the 'look ahead and plan' attention.

**Failure mode:** If load balancing lags reality, a sector saturates and the controller loses the picture before help arrives — the structural fix has a reaction-time cost. And splitting/combining sectors itself creates a coordination-handoff burden at exactly the busy moment, so the act of adding capacity transiently consumes capacity.

## What this field knows
ATC is the most mature working example of a single human exercising judgment over a machine that perceives vastly more than they can — radar/ADS-B tracking thousands of trajectories, projecting them forward in time, and surfacing only the handful that demand action. The field's deepest, hard-won insight is that the scarce resource is not information but the controller's *picture* — a continuously-held mental model of who is where, going where, and intending what. Every artifact and doctrine is engineered around protecting and feeding that picture, NOT around displaying status. Four principles transfer hardest: (1) Tiered tempo of alerting — the system separates "you have minutes to think" (medium-term, advisory) from "act NOW" (short-term, commanding), and never lets the second category get diluted by the first; nuisance alerts are treated as a safety defect, not noise to tolerate. (2) Intent must be made machine-readable, not inferred — the controller's plan lives in an artifact (the strip / the cleared flight level) that BOTH human and machine read from and write to, so the machine checks conflicts against what the human INTENDS, not just what is currently happening. (3) The unit of action is a single, closed, acknowledged instruction — clearances are atomic, read back, and verified, so a loop is provably closed rather than assumed. (4) Handoff is explicit and physical — responsibility for a trajectory is transferred through a deliberate ritual (strip passing / "your control"), never ambiguous about who owns the decision. The field also knows the costs: alert fatigue, automation complacency, "head-down" tunneling on the screen, and the danger of the machine being confidently wrong about intent. The whole discipline is a 70-year refinement of "let only conflicts rise, share intent both ways, and make every action a closed loop."
