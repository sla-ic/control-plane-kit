## Abstraction hierarchy — model the work domain as a 5-level means-ends ladder before designing any display. The levels: (1) Functional Purpose (why the system exists / safety goals that must never be violated, e.g. 'keep the core covered and cooled'), (2) Abstract Function (mass/energy/momentum balances — the conservation laws), (3) Generalized Function (processes: heat transfer, flow loops), (4) Physical Function (pumps, valves, their capabilities), (5) Physical Form (where the thing physically is). Each level answers WHY (level above) and HOW (level below). What rises to the human is keyed to the level their decision lives at — diagnosis happens at the top, action at the bottom.

**Term of art:** Abstraction hierarchy (Rasmussen) / Work Domain Analysis

Rasmussen's insight: experts move fluidly UP the ladder when something breaks ('the pump tripped' -> 'so heat removal is failing' -> 'so the energy-balance goal is threatened'). A display that only shows physical form (pump on/off lights) forces the human to climb the ladder mentally under stress — exactly TMI. EID makes the higher levels directly visible so the climb is perceptual, not computational. The hierarchy is built first as an analysis artifact; the display is derived from it. This is the field's answer to 'what should rise': not the data, but the GOAL-STATE the data implies, at the altitude of the pending decision.

**Failure mode:** Single Sensor–Single Indicator (SS-SI) design: one gauge per sensor, the pre-TMI default. Every variable is shown at the physical-form level only, so the human must integrate dozens of readings into a goal-state in their head. Scales to thousands of indicators (TMI had 1,900) and guarantees overload because the integration work is dumped on the scarcest resource.

## Ecological Interface Design (EID) — render the work-domain constraints as emergent perceptual forms (geometric shapes, polygons, configural displays) so that 'normal' looks like a recognizable Gestalt and ANY abnormality distorts the shape pre-attentively. The human reads system health as a single visual percept rather than scanning N dials. Classic instance: a polygon whose vertices are tied to related parameters — when balanced, it's a symmetric shape; one parameter drifting warps it visibly across the room.

**Term of art:** Configural / polygon display; emergent perceptual feature

Exploits the human perceptual system's massive parallel pattern-detection: a broken symmetry or a crossed line 'pops out' with zero deliberate search, offloading attention. The form is engineered so that the visual emergent feature MAPS to a real domain constraint (e.g. mass balance), so what you perceive is literally the safety property, not a decoration. Validated to cut response time and error rate and to support diagnosing UNANTICIPATED faults — the TMI failure class — because the display shows constraint violations the designers never enumerated as specific alarms.

**Failure mode:** Two known traps: (1) emergent-feature displays become unreadable when many are shown without consistent shared visual grammar — the Gestalts collide; (2) the mapping can be 'salient but wrong' — if the visual feature that pops out doesn't correspond to the most safety-relevant variable, you've made an irrelevant thing loud. Also requires training: naive operators match but don't beat traditional displays until experienced.

## Dark-board / dark-and-quiet doctrine — design so that a plant operating normally produces NO active alarms and NO illuminated annunciators. The board is dark by design. Health is encoded as ABSENCE; the appearance of anything is itself the message. Standing alarms, 'nuisance' alarms, and chronically-lit tiles are treated as defects to be eliminated, because they destroy the signal value of illumination.

**Term of art:** Dark board / darkboard configuration; nuisance-alarm & standing-alarm suppression

Restores information content to each alert: when nothing lights during normal ops, a single new light is unambiguous and high-value. Directly counters TMI, where alarms were always partly lit and an avalanche of 750 identical tiles carried near-zero discriminating information. The doctrine forces designers to define 'normal' tightly and suppress everything inside it, so the human's attention is spent ONLY on genuine deviation. Interestingly, operators were observed repurposing alarms as a normal-ops monitoring/to-do aid — revealing that humans WILL mine any channel for situation awareness, which designers must anticipate.

**Failure mode:** Alarm flooding / annunciator avalanche: when a real upset hits, hundreds of correlated alarms fire in seconds (one root cause cascades through every downstream sensor), exceeding human processing bandwidth precisely when stakes peak. The cure spawned its own discipline (alarm rationalization, EEMUA-191-style rate limits) because naive 'one condition = one alarm' produces unusable floods.

## Critical Safety Function (CSF) status trees + function-based Emergency Operating Procedures — reduce ALL plant states to a small fixed set of safety functions that must be maintained (subcriticality, core cooling, heat sink, RCS integrity, containment, inventory). Continuously evaluated status trees show each function as GREEN/YELLOW/ORANGE/RED. The operator monitors ~6 goals, not thousands of variables, and the tree tells them which function is threatened and routes them to the right procedure — regardless of what caused it.

**Term of art:** Critical Safety Function status tree; symptom-based / function-based EOPs

Pivots the whole information architecture from EVENT-based ('is this a LOCA? a steam-line break?') to SYMPTOM/FUNCTION-based ('is the core being cooled — yes or no?'). This is the direct fix for TMI's fatal misdiagnosis: operators don't have to correctly name the accident to act correctly, they just have to keep each safety function in the green. Collapses an unbounded space of scenarios into a bounded set of goal-states, which is what makes it tractable for one human. The tree is the contract for what rises: a function leaving green is the event.

**Failure mode:** Pre-TMI event-based procedures required correct diagnosis FIRST — if the operator picked the wrong accident scenario (as at TMI, where a LOCA was read as the opposite), every subsequent 'correct' action made it worse. Diagnosis-first architectures fail catastrophically on unanticipated or compound events.

## Safety Parameter Display System (SPDS) — a single NRC-mandated dedicated display (NUREG-0737) that continuously aggregates the handful of parameters defining plant safety status into ONE consolidated view, so an operator (or the Shift Technical Advisor) can assess overall safety state at a glance without reconstructing it from scattered panels. It is a deliberate 'one pane that answers: are we safe?' layered ON TOP of the full instrumentation, not replacing it.

**Term of art:** SPDS (Safety Parameter Display System); parameter-vs-limit / deviation-bar display

Implements the principle that the highest-altitude question ('is the plant safe right now?') deserves its own always-present, purpose-built channel rather than being an emergent conclusion the human must assemble. Maps directly to the CSF set. The design lesson learned the hard way: early SPDS versions just threw key parameters on a screen and still made the operator interpret them — later versions added deviation bars and parameter-vs-LIMIT representations so the display shows the MARGIN to the safety boundary, not just the value.

**Failure mode:** A consolidated 'status' view that shows raw values without their limits/margins reproduces the original problem one level up — the human still has to remember every setpoint and compute proximity to danger. Showing value-in-context-of-limit is the load-bearing detail; showing value alone is theater.

## Skill/Rule/Knowledge (SRK) framework — explicitly classify every operator task by the cognitive control level it demands: Skill-based (automatic sensorimotor, e.g. tracking a needle), Rule-based (if-then procedure recall), Knowledge-based (novel reasoning from first principles when no rule fits). Design then ROUTES work: keep routine monitoring perceptual-motor, surface rules as just-in-time procedures, and ferociously protect the human's tiny knowledge-based capacity for true novelty.

**Term of art:** SRK taxonomy (Rasmussen); skill-/rule-/knowledge-based behavior

Recognizes the human's reasoning bandwidth is the genuine scarce resource and that knowledge-based behavior is slow, effortful, and error-prone under stress. The interface's job is to handle as much as possible at the skill/rule levels (cheap, fast, reliable) so that when a genuinely novel situation arrives, the human arrives at it UNFATIGUED and with attention free. Also predicts the error TYPES at each level (slips/lapses at skill-rule vs mistakes at knowledge level), so designers know which safeguard to deploy where.

**Failure mode:** Forcing knowledge-based reasoning for routine work (TMI: operators reasoning from scratch about valve state because no display gave it directly) burns the scarce resource on the mundane, so none is left for the actual emergency. Conversely, 'strong but wrong' rule-based responses fire familiar procedures in unfamiliar situations — the human pattern-matches to the wrong rule and executes it confidently.

## Status indication must reflect ACTUAL measured state, never the command issued — codified as a hard human-factors rule after TMI. A control that was told to close must not show 'closed'; it must show what a sensor on the actual component reports. Absence of a signal must never be allowed to mean 'all fine.' Feedback is required to close on the physical world, not on the controller's intent.

**Term of art:** Position/state feedback vs command feedback; 'absence of signal is not confirmation'

TMI's defining deception: the PORV relief valve's light went dark when the CLOSE signal was sent, while the valve was physically stuck OPEN, draining coolant for hours. Operators trusted the light. The rule forces the loop to close on ground truth, so the human's mental model is anchored to reality, not to the system's belief about itself. This is the field's most quotable transfer: an agent reporting 'done' (command sent) versus 'verified done' (state confirmed) are different signals, and conflating them is how a human is confidently steered into disaster.

**Failure mode:** Command-as-status: the interface reports the instruction it issued as if it were the outcome achieved. The human cannot distinguish 'I asked' from 'it happened,' and silently diverges from reality. The more reliable the system USUALLY is, the more dangerous this becomes, because trust is calibrated to the common case.

## Alarm rationalization, prioritization & shelving — a formal pre-operational process where every potential alarm must justify its existence against criteria (Does it require operator ACTION? Is there a defined response? Is it timely?), is assigned a priority by consequence-and-time-to-act, and is suppressible/shelvable under defined conditions (e.g. dynamic suppression of downstream alarms once the root-cause alarm fires). Alarm RATES are budgeted (target alarms/10-min in normal ops and during upsets).

**Term of art:** Alarm rationalization; priority-by-consequence-and-time; dynamic/state-based suppression; alarm shelving (EEMUA 191 / ISA-18.2 lineage)

Treats the right to interrupt the human as a scarce, rationed budget rather than a free action any subsystem can take. An alarm earns its place only if it maps to an operator decision/action — pure 'FYI' notifications are designed OUT. State-based suppression collapses the cascade: when a pump trips, the 40 consequential alarms it would trigger are auto-suppressed in favor of the one causal alarm. This is the field's machinery for keeping the dark-board promise alive during real upsets, not just normal ops.

**Failure mode:** Alarm-as-information vs alarm-as-call-to-action confusion: when alarms are used to convey status rather than demand action, the action-required signals drown in the FYI noise. Also 'alarm normalization' — operators chronically silencing/ignoring a flood until they tune out a real one (the avalanche made every alarm cry wolf).

## Independent human cross-check roles layered onto the interface: the Shift Technical Advisor (STA) — a degreed engineer added post-TMI whose ONLY job is to monitor overall safety-function status from a diagnostic/abstract altitude while the at-the-controls operators handle moment-to-moment actions — plus two-person concurrent verification and peer-check/'three-way communication' for critical actions.

**Term of art:** Shift Technical Advisor (STA); independent/concurrent verification; three-way communication; peer-checking

Deliberately SPLITS attention across humans by altitude: the operators live at the action level (rule/skill), the STA lives at the goal/diagnosis level (knowledge), so no single human has to simultaneously fly the plane and diagnose the engine. The STA reads the SPDS/CSF trees, not the individual controls. This is the field's recognition that 'one human + firehose' sometimes needs a second human positioned explicitly at a DIFFERENT level of abstraction, with a handoff protocol between them — joint loop-closing across roles, not one overloaded node.

**Failure mode:** Without role separation by altitude, the diagnostic 'step back and ask if we're safe' never happens because everyone is heads-down executing — TMI operators were so consumed managing indications they never reframed to 'is the core covered?' Conversely, poorly bounded verification creates diffusion of responsibility (everyone assumes someone else checked).

## NUREG-0700 Human-System Interface Design Review — a formal, auditable, regulator-enforced review process where the interface itself is a safety-rated artifact subject to documented design-review against explicit human-factors criteria, with the alarm system, SPDS, group-view displays, computer-based procedures, soft controls, and automation each as a named review category. Plants cannot operate without passing it; design choices about what rises to the operator are traceable and accountable.

**Term of art:** NUREG-0700 (HSI Design Review Guidelines); Control Room Design Review (CRDR); TMI Action Plan (NUREG-0660 / -0737)

Institutionalizes attention-management as engineering subject to inspection rather than designer taste. The Control Room Design Review (born NUREG-0700, 1981) required operating plants to systematically re-examine every panel against human-factors principles and FIX deficiencies — converting 'good UX' into a licensing gate. The deep transfer: in a domain where a missed signal kills, the decision of what reaches the human is too important to be implicit or ad hoc; it must be specified, justified, reviewed, and version-controlled like any other safety system.

**Failure mode:** The pre-TMI baseline NUREG/CR-1270 documented: 'human engineering planning was virtually nonexistent' — interfaces accreted ad hoc, panel by panel, with no one accountable for the integrated cognitive load. Unreviewed, unbudgeted interface growth is the default failure, and it is invisible until the upset that needs all 1,900 indicators at once.

## Function-allocation & levels-of-automation analysis — explicitly decide, for each function, WHO does it (human, automation, or shared) and at WHAT level of automation, then design the interface to keep the human 'in the loop' on automated functions via continuous feedback so authority can hand back cleanly when automation hits its boundary.

**Term of art:** Function allocation; levels of automation (LOA); 'out-of-the-loop' performance problem; automation transparency

Confronts the automation paradox head-on: more automation handles more, but leaves the human with the rare, hard residual cases AND a degraded mental model from not actively flying. The field's countermeasure is to keep automation TRANSPARENT (show what it's doing and WHY, show its limits approaching) so the human is a calibrated supervisor, not a startled bystander at handoff. Decides deliberately which loops the human must stay warm on versus which can run dark, rather than maximizing automation and hoping the human catches the exceptions.

**Failure mode:** Out-of-the-loop unfamiliarity / automation surprise: when automation silently handles everything then suddenly dumps a degraded, half-understood situation on a human who lost situation awareness and mode awareness ('what was it doing? what state are we in now?'). The handoff arrives at the worst moment with the least context — the inverse of joint loop-closing.

## What this field knows
This field's hard-won, transferable knowledge is that an interface can drown a human while the underlying system fails catastrophically — and that the cure is NOT "show less" or "rank alerts better." TMI's operators were not under-informed; they were over-informed and mis-structured. ~750 identical alarms fired, a valve-position light showed the COMMAND SENT rather than the VALVE'S ACTUAL STATE, and 1,900 panel displays had near-zero human-engineering planning. The deep lesson the field extracted over 40 years: what should rise to a human is not raw data, not even prioritized data, but the STATE OF THE GOALS THE HUMAN IS RESPONSIBLE FOR, expressed in the structure of the work itself. Its signature move (Rasmussen/Vicente's abstraction hierarchy + ecological interface design) is to first model the work domain as a means-ends ladder — from physical components up through functions up to the purposes/safety-goals that must never be violated — and then build displays that make the higher-purpose levels DIRECTLY PERCEIVABLE rather than something the human must compute under stress. It distinguishes three levels of cognitive control (skill/rule/knowledge-based behavior) and deliberately designs so that routine work stays perceptual-motor, rules are surfaced as procedures, and the human's scarce knowledge-based reasoning is reserved for genuine novelty. Crucially, it inverts the default of status displays: the "dark board" doctrine says a correctly-running plant should show NOTHING — silence IS the signal of health — so that any illumination carries real information. It also codified a transfer-of-authority discipline (function-based EOPs, critical safety function status trees, shift technical advisor, two-person verification) for how human and automation hand control back and forth without either dropping the loop. The field uniquely knows how to engineer attention as a safety-rated, auditable, regulated artifact — not a UX nicety — under firehose data where the cost of a missed loop is a meltdown.
