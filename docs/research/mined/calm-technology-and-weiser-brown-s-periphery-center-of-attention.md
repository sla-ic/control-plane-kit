# Calm technology and Weiser & Brown's periphery/center of attention
> Mined via open-learning re-run (Sonnet).

## Peripheral Display as Ambient Notification
**Term of art:** Peripheral display / ambient display
Weiser and Brown's 1996 paper "Designing Calm Technology" introduced the notion that good systems move information between the periphery and center of a person's attention without demanding the person actively pull it. The canonical artifact was the "Dangling String" — a plastic strip connected to a network cable that wiggled faster when network traffic increased. A person in the room could sense busyness without looking at a dashboard, and could choose to focus on it only when the wiggle became unusual. The mechanism is encoding state in a sensory channel (motion, sound, light level) that the human nervous system monitors pre-attentively, so the cost of staying informed is near zero until something warrants focal attention.
**Failure mode:** Habituation. When the ambient signal is always present and never consequential, the nervous system learns to fully suppress it (like the hum of an air conditioner). The display stops being peripheral and becomes invisible noise — the exact opposite of its intention.

## Encalming Through Legible State Transitions
**Term of art:** Calm / uncalm boundary crossing
Weiser and Brown described the ideal system as one that can move smoothly across the center-to-periphery spectrum — but the critical design moment is the transition itself. A calm technology makes the boundary crossing legible: a glance at the periphery lets you judge whether the thing now needs to move to center. The InfoCanvas research tradition (University of Alberta, early 2000s) operationalized this as "glanceability" — a display was graded by whether a five-second look was sufficient to determine whether deeper investigation was warranted, no more. The mechanism is designing information density and visual hierarchy so that the anomaly pops out pre-attentively (using Gestalt figure-ground, motion, or color saturation change) while baseline state recedes.
**Failure mode:** False urgency. If the visual pop-out fires on non-actionable events, the human trains themselves to ignore it. The failure mode is the same as a car alarm nobody looks up for — the cue loses its signal value through false positives.

## Feedthrough as Coordination Artifact
**Term of art:** Feedthrough (from CSCW, Dourish & Bellotti 1992)
In shared-workspace research the term "feedthrough" names the information that leaks from one actor's actions to another actor's awareness without explicit communication. In calm technology this is repurposed: the system's ongoing work becomes feedthrough that the human perceives at the periphery, giving them awareness of system state without requiring a status report. A well-designed feedthrough lets the human know the system is working, is not stuck, and is handling what they delegated — reducing the anxiety that drives unnecessary check-ins. The mechanism is making system behavior legible as a byproduct of the system doing its job, not as a separate reporting layer.
**Failure mode:** Opacity masquerading as calm. A system that is too quiet — emitting no feedthrough — looks the same whether it is working well or has failed silently. The human has no peripheral signal to notice when something has gone wrong, so failures surface only when they become crises.

## Notification Coalescing and the Glanceable Summary
**Term of art:** Notification coalescing / digest mode
In both iOS/Android notification design and in research systems like Kimchi (CMU, early 2000s), individual events are batched into a periodic, scannable summary rather than each firing an interrupt. The mechanism separates the event stream (continuous, system-paced) from the human's attention rhythm (bursty, task-gated). A coalesced notification presents N items as a single attentional unit; the human can choose to expand or act on a single pass. This is the system taking on the integration burden — counting, grouping, surfacing only the envelope — rather than routing every raw event.
**Failure mode:** Time-sensitivity collapse. Coalescing works for low-urgency streams but fails when any item in the batch is time-critical. If the digest arrives 15 minutes after an event needed action, calm became negligence. The design challenge is correctly classifying what can wait for a digest versus what must interrupt.

## Progressive Disclosure of Causal Depth
**Term of art:** Progressive disclosure (Nielsen, 1990s; reapplied in ambient/calm contexts)
The calm technology version of progressive disclosure is layered legibility of cause: the periphery shows outcome state ("something changed"), one deliberate glance shows magnitude ("changed significantly"), one tap or click shows cause ("three tickets re-prioritized by the PM"). The human decides at each layer whether to invest further attention. In the Microsoft Research SideShow and similar glanceable-secondary-display work, this was implemented as a physical hierarchy — wristband vibrates, phone lock screen shows count, app shows detail — where each level requires a more deliberate physical gesture, metering attentional cost to information depth.
**Failure mode:** Depth collapse. When the peripheral layer omits enough context to judge whether to go deeper, the human must always go to the bottom layer to be sure — the system has not earned trust that the periphery is a reliable filter, so every peripheral cue triggers a full investigation. This defeats the bandwidth savings entirely.

## Rhythm and the Heartbeat Signal
**Term of art:** Heartbeat / liveness signal
In calm technology and in ambient display research (e.g., the Ambient Orb product line, Good et al.), systems establish a regular, low-cost signal that tells the human the system is alive and watching. The Ambient Orb used slow color shifts driven by stock price or weather — the motion itself was a heartbeat asserting liveness. In industrial process control the equivalent is the "watchdog timer" whose absence is itself an alarm. The design principle is that the human's peripheral nervous system can monitor a regular rhythm cheaply, and deviation from that rhythm is what triggers focal attention. The mechanism turns the absence of anomaly into positive confirmation, not merely the absence of a negative signal.
**Failure mode:** Heartbeat gaming. If the heartbeat is generated by a monitoring process separate from the actual working process, a failure in the working process can go undetected while the heartbeat continues. The human's peripheral system sees calm while the actual system has failed — the signal becomes misleading rather than informative.

## Horizon of Attention and the Periphery Contract
**Term of art:** Horizon of attention (Weiser & Brown term); periphery contract (not Weiser's term — the design community's operationalization)
Weiser and Brown described information as existing on a "horizon" — nearer information is more attended, far information is pre-attentive. The practical design artifact that emerged from this was what practitioners call the periphery contract: an explicit or implicit agreement about what class of information lives at which distance, and what movement across the horizon means. In the ambient display tradition, this was often materialized as a physical object placed in the room — a lamp, a stream of bubbles, a piece of kinetic art — whose sole job was to hold one information variable at the periphery. The contract is: this object lives in your environment; if it changes dramatically, it has earned your glance; if you glance and it was not worth it, the contract is broken.
**Failure mode:** Contract violation through scope creep. When the peripheral artifact is asked to carry multiple variables or is updated too frequently, the human can no longer form a stable expectation of what it means. The horizon collapses — everything is equidistant and equally unread.

## What this field knows
Calm technology uniquely understands that human attention is not binary (noticed / not noticed) but exists on a continuous spectrum from pre-attentive perception to deep focal engagement, and that a system's job is to live at the right point on that spectrum for the current situation — and to move gracefully when the situation changes. The field's deepest insight is that the cost of staying informed must be paid in perceptual bandwidth, not in deliberate cognitive acts, and that failures of calm design are almost always failures to honor that cost asymmetry. It also knows that the periphery is not merely a holding area for low-priority information but an active trust-building channel: a peripheral signal that reliably predicts when focal attention is warranted earns the human's willingness to act on it, while a peripheral signal that misfires trains the human to ignore everything.
