# Aircrew and ICU checklists / Pronovost-style protocols
> Mined via open-learning re-run (Sonnet).

## Challenge-and-Response Checklist
**Term of art:** Challenge-and-response (C&R) checklist
The first officer reads a checklist item aloud (the challenge); the pilot completing the action responds verbally and physically confirms the state (the response) before the pair advances. Neither party may advance the list unilaterally. The checklist is not a reminder to do things — it is a joint verification that things have been done, which is a critical distinction. Items are deliberately terse because the pilots already know how to perform each action; the checklist's function is to interrupt the flow state and force deliberate confirmation.
**Failure mode:** Checklist capture — crews read through items mechanically without verifying actual state, converting a verification ritual into a recitation ritual. Guarded against: completing a critical configuration step from memory alone and moving on before actual system state is confirmed.

## Sterile Cockpit Rule
**Term of art:** Sterile cockpit (FAR 91.105 / airline ops specs)
Below 10,000 feet or during critical phases of flight, all non-essential communication is prohibited. Crew members may not discuss non-flight topics, and ATC communications are kept to operational necessity. The rule artificially narrows the attentional aperture at the moments when the environment (proximity to terrain, traffic density, configuration changes) demands it most. The boundary between sterile and non-sterile is itself announced — "sterile cockpit" — so both crew members know the mode.
**Failure mode:** Crew members violating sterile cockpit socially (not wanting to appear rude) during non-safety but still task-dense moments; or the rule creates silence that masks genuine alerts because crew doesn't want to break protocol to surface a concern. Guarded against: attentional fragmentation at peak-load moments.

## Crew Resource Management (CRM) Gradient Authority
**Term of art:** CRM / authority gradient / assertiveness training
CRM training explicitly teaches junior crew members to override social hierarchy when they hold safety-critical information. A specific escalating verbal script — inquiry, advocacy, assertion, command — is drilled so that a first officer who observes a deviation has a ritualized path to override the captain. The gradient is managed, not flattened: the captain retains authority in non-critical moments, but the protocol creates a sanctioned path for the lower-status actor to stop the system.
**Failure mode:** Training works in simulators and fails in aircraft because actual authority gradients (seniority, cultural deference) suppress use of the assertiveness scripts. Guarded against: a high-information, low-status actor being unable to surface a critical signal through social friction alone.

## Pronovost Central Line Bundle
**Term of art:** Bundled intervention / checklist-as-stopping-rule
Peter Pronovost's 2001 ICU protocol condensed central-line insertion into five evidence-based steps and gave nurses explicit authority to stop a physician from proceeding if any step was skipped. The checklist was not advisory — it was a stopping rule. The authority-to-stop was the mechanism innovation, not the list itself (the list was already known). Nurses recorded compliance; data fed back to unit leadership monthly. In the Michigan Keystone project this reduced CLABSI rates by two-thirds across 103 ICUs over 18 months.
**Failure mode:** Physicians treating the checklist as documentation rather than constraint — completing the paper after the procedure rather than verifying before. The stopping authority degrades when nurses lack organizational backing to enforce it against senior physicians. Guarded against: expert overconfidence in high-skill, routine tasks where familiarity breeds skipped verification.

## Crew Briefing / Threat and Error Management (TEM) Pre-flight
**Term of art:** Threat and error management (TEM) briefing
Before each flight, the crew explicitly names anticipated threats (weather, known NOTAMs, unfamiliar airport, fatigue state) and pre-commits to specific mitigations. This is prospective rather than reactive: the crew is constructing a shared mental model of the flight's threat profile before entering it, so that when a named threat materializes mid-flight, the response is recognized pattern rather than novel problem. The ritual forces the captain to narrate their reasoning so the first officer can correct it before flight begins.
**Failure mode:** Briefings become rote — same words each flight regardless of actual threat profile — because deviation from the script feels like admitting a problem. Guarded against: a crew entering a threat environment with asymmetric awareness (captain knows the threat, first officer does not).

## Read-Back / Hear-Back Protocol
**Term of art:** Read-back (ATC) / hear-back
When ATC issues a clearance, the pilot reads it back verbatim; ATC listens for the read-back and corrects it if wrong. The pilot cannot begin execution until ATC has either confirmed or corrected the read-back. The loop is: transmit → receive → echo → verify → execute. This is a closed-loop communication protocol: information doesn't count as transferred until its receipt is confirmed by the receiver's demonstration of accurate encoding. The failure is not in transmission but in the verification of comprehension.
**Failure mode:** Hear-back failure — ATC misses an incorrect read-back because of frequency congestion or workload, confirming a wrong clearance. Guarded against: a pilot executing a clearance they heard incorrectly, with no mechanism for ATC to detect the divergence before the aircraft moves.

## Daily Goals Sheet (Pronovost ICU Rounding)
**Term of art:** Daily goals sheet / ICU daily goal rounds
Pronovost introduced a structured one-page form completed each morning during rounds that required each team member to articulate what the patient's goal was for that day and whether the team had a shared plan to achieve it. The resident, nurse, and attending each answered: "Do you understand the patient's goals for today?" Misalignment surfaced as divergent answers. The form forced explicit goal-setting rather than allowing vague shared understanding, and it named the responsible party for each action by end of day.
**Failure mode:** The form becomes a rounding ritual rather than a genuine alignment mechanism — teams complete it without surfacing actual disagreements, because disagreement in a rounding setting feels like conflict. Guarded against: ICU teams with locally coherent but globally divergent plans for the same patient.

## Callouts and Verbal Confirmations During Approach (ALAR Practices)
**Term of art:** Approach-and-landing callouts / ALAR (Approach and Landing Accident Reduction)
During approach, both crew members make scripted verbal callouts at defined altitude gates: "1,000 feet, stable, landing" or "500 feet, unstable, go-around." These are not optional commentary — they are binary decision points with pre-committed thresholds. If the aircraft is not in defined stable configuration by 500 feet, the go-around is automatic, not negotiated. The verbal callout converts an analog, continuous situation (is this approach good enough?) into a discrete, pre-committed decision at a named gate. This removes the decision from the moment of highest workload and highest ambiguity.
**Failure mode:** Continuation bias — crews below the gate announce "unstable" but continue the approach because the runway is in sight and stopping feels like failure. Guarded against: the human tendency to accept degrading conditions incrementally rather than recognize a threshold crossing.

## What this field knows
Aircrew and ICU protocol design discovered that the bottleneck is not information or skill — crews and physicians already have both — but the human tendency to treat familiar routines as verified. The field's core invention is forcing explicit, verbal, often-dyadic confirmation at specifically chosen moments rather than allowing assumed shared understanding to stand. The deepest insight is that authority to stop must be institutionally granted to the lower-status actor, because the highest-information signal at a critical moment often comes from a junior person who has no natural path to halt an expert. The field also learned that the failure mode of any protocol is ritual capture: forms and callouts that are executed correctly but no longer perform verification — making the protocol itself a source of false confidence.
